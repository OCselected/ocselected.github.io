China Open Source Daily — 2026-11-06

The public consultation on MOFCOM and MOST Announcement No. 48 closes on 2026-11-15, and the draft’s answer to the question Chinese open-weight labs have been waiting for turns out to be a legal construction rather than an exemption. Annex 1 excludes technologies that have already entered the public domain ’through open source, academic publication or other means’ — but the same instrument that creates the carve-out has already decided that open-sourcing is itself a technology export subject to pre-export registration. The interim registration has been live since 2026-10-16, and legal analysis published by Fangda on 2026-10-20 reads its third trigger — a change in the owner or actual controller of domestic technology to an overseas entity — as a deemed-export rule that acts without any movement of technology across a border, and identifies automatic licensing for restricted AI technologies in five named scenarios including open sourcing, building one’s own overseas compute centre, overseas tape-out, joint R&D using foreign resources, and international standards participation, with Article 72 of the Foreign Trade Law carrying a RMB 50,000 ceiling for an unregistered free-technology contract against the heavier penalties for unlicensed restricted-technology export. Ten days later, at Wuzhen, the three labs that use that route most — DeepSeek, Alibaba’s Qwen and MiniMax — described the regime they are about to be governed by: Liu Dayiheng named non-recallability and the absence of an external perimeter; Gao Xi said his colleagues were researching model tiering; Wang Jian said the premise of openness is trustworthiness, controllability and governability. The 2026-11-03 NDRC briefing, reported by Bloomberg, described lifecycle safety standards with incident disclosure and a transition period. Read together, four artefacts from four institutions in three weeks describe a governance regime assembled out of obligations the releasing firm can assert about itself — where the public good is a security commons that no ministry has been funded to operate.

China Open Source Daily — 2026-11-05

A Reuters report on 2026-10-20 described openEuler as a distro with a desktop story; four days later the project’s kernel repository quietly answered a more interesting question. The canonic home of openEuler’s kernel moved from Gitee to AtomGit — the source-forge arm of the same industrial stack — while a mirror named openeuler-mirror on GitHub kept publishing, last pushed 2026-11-04, with no issues, no pull requests, 59 stars, and 1,245,880 commits behind it. Reading that mirror’s most recent 400 commits against AtomGit’s landing page produces two pieces of evidence that point in opposite directions. From GitHub’s side (the only side that can be API-queried mechanically) 59 of the last 100 commits are individual, non-CI patches — 45 authored by foreign-mainline maintainers (Google, Meta, NVIDIA, SUSE, AMD, Intel) — and 55 of 100 carry a From: @... tag that reveals their real path was an !NNNNN merge request on the openEuler forge, reproduced through a bot that signs off as Tengda Wu <wutengda2@huawei.com>. From AtomGit’s side: 1,849 issues, 1,486 pull requests, 203 members — while GitHub’s mirror of the same repository holds exactly zero of each. The 2026-09-30 release, openEuler 26.09, credits 834 contributing developers. The resulting picture is neither fake open source nor an ordinary upstream: a genuinely open downstream that is a locus of decision inside China’s procurement market and a mirror everywhere else — and a divergence risk that is visibly small today and structurally large tomorrow.

China Open Source Daily — 2026-11-04

The 2026-11-03 Wuzhen programme produced a theme, a hall, a ministers’ meeting and a set of corporate speeches; a day later it is still true that not one of the day’s open-source headlines is a licence. The checkable record sits one layer down, in the repositories and model cards, and read together it says something sharper than either the speeches or October’s export-control reporting. ZCode — the coding agent Zhipu AI open-sourced under Apache-2.0 on 2026-09-20 after it had silently uploaded users’ git histories to Alibaba Cloud OSS — published v3.17.2 on 2026-11-03 through a bot commit that replaced a single file, 9,635 lines in, 1,472 lines out, for a diff of 11,107 lines in one commit message that says ‘chore’. Its contributor list is three names, it turned issues off, and in forty-four days it has merged no pull request from anyone outside the company that owns it. Across the same organisations, every published artefact carries a conditional licence: Apache-2.0 for the harness, MIT for GLM-5.3-Flash, and something called the Kimi K3 License and the GLM-5.3 License for the frontier weights — terms that track revenue, monthly users and monthly revenue, and in GLM-5.3’s case a mandatory ‘security review’ whose scope is set by the vendor. Meanwhile the zai-org GitHub record shows the shape of publication the sector actually runs: GLM-5 logged sixty-three commits in eight months, fourteen merges and 17 open issues — nearly all documentation — while its 2.36-million-download weights live on Hugging Face, which produced fifteen commits in the same window and no consumer capability at all. The institution being built is publication without custodianship: an unconditional grant, in an index, priced by download counts, with the maintenance surface rented out to a hosting platform and the terms of the grant written by the grantor.

China Open Source Daily — 2026-11-03

On 2026-11-02 the Wuzhen Summit opened under a theme in which the word open source appears for the first time in twelve years of the conference: ‘Open Source and Openness, Joint Contribution and Shared Benefits.’ The same day, the World Internet Conference’s AI Professional Committee held its annual plenary and approved its 2027 plan, whose one operative sentence is that the committee will ‘actively promote the transformation of the open-source openness initiative into action’ (积极推进开源开放倡议转化为行动). Read together with the objects the summit has actually built — a first-ever open-source-themed experience hall in the World Internet Science and Technology Museum, an open-source track in the Straight to Wuzhen competition, a Wuzhen Talk open-source youth forum opened to public booking, and 1,000-plus achievements across 671 exhibitors from 54 countries — the picture is of a state promoting open source as a norm it issues rather than a repository it publishes. The instrument is the summit theme and the committee work plan; the missing instrument is a licence, a deposit, or an access rule. Ant Group’s contribution to the opening of the experience hall states the ambition in its own words: ‘we look forward to open source becoming the basic public good of the digital world’ — a claim about a commons made by a firm, in a hall built by a conference, in a country whose largest open-source foundation runs on membership dues rather than endowments.
