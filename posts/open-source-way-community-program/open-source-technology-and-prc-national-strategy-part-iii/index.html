Open-Source Technology and PRC National Strategy: Part III#

The Great Divergence: Two Years That Fractured the Digital Commons#

Author: 适兕（Kuosi）— The Way of Open Source (开源之道) Date: July 4, 2026 Series Note: This is an independent third installment, continuing the analysis begun by Sunny Cheung in Parts I (May 13, 2024) and II (June 2, 2024) for the Jamestown Foundation’s China Brief. The original series examined the OpenAtom Foundation, OpenHarmony, and the military-civil fusion strategy. This installment extends the analysis through mid-2026, situating these developments within the broader phenomenon of the “Great Divergence” between native and localized open-source ecosystems.

Executive Summary#

  • Two years after Cheung’s analysis, the bifurcation of global open source has accelerated beyond mere “technological independence” into a structural Great Divergence: two fundamentally incompatible models of open source now operate in parallel ecosystems with minimal cross-contamination.
  • OpenAtom Foundation’s flagship projects (OpenHarmony, openEuler) have achieved significant domestic penetration but remain effectively quarantined from the global open-source mainstream—not by technical inferiority, but by institutional mistrust rooted in their governance architecture.
  • The rise of AI coding agents (Claude Code, Codex CLI, Hermes Agent) and the “Loop Engineering” paradigm has introduced a third force: algorithmic governance of software production. This shifts the battleground from operating systems to the agentic layer that generates code.
  • China’s response—Gitee’s agent-native features, the OpenAtom AI special interest group, and state-directed LLM training programs—represents a strategic adaptation, but one that operates within the same institutional constraints Cheung identified in 2024.
  • The GFW has evolved from a passive censorship infrastructure into an active protocol-level agent: its intermittent, targeted disruption of Western research resources (arXiv, Semantic Scholar, publisher PDF servers) now functions as a de facto information firewall that systematically disadvantages localized open-source development.
  • The “Single Digital Market” that native open source presupposes no longer exists. What remains are two gravitational fields, each with its own rules of trust, value measurement, and governance legitimacy.

Introduction: Beyond “Technological Independence”#

When Sunny Cheung documented the OpenAtom Foundation’s state-aligned governance in May 2024, the dominant frame was technological sovereignty—China’s drive to reduce dependence on Western platforms. OpenHarmony was a hedge against Google’s Android; openEuler was a hedge against Red Hat; RISC-V was a hedge against ARM and x86.

Twenty-six months later, the story is no longer about hedging. It is about divergence.

The concept, borrowed from economic history (Pomeranz, 2000) and adapted by the Chinese open-source analysis collective “开源之道” (The Way of Open Source), posits that when a collaborative meme (open source) crosses a fundamental institutional boundary—from a rule-of-law, hacker-ethic, global-commons environment into a bureaucratic, state-controlled, security-prioritizing environment—it undergoes an obligate mutation. The resulting organism shares the same name as its ancestor but operates on entirely different principles.

This installment documents that mutation in its active phase, examining three domains Cheung’s original series could not have anticipated: the agentic AI revolution in software production, the maturation of China’s parallel open-source infrastructure, and the deepening of the institutional quarantine that now separates the two ecosystems.

I. The Agentic Revolution and the Third Layer#

I.1 The Loop Engineering Paradigm#

The most significant development in global open-source production since mid-2024 is the emergence of Loop Engineering—the practice of designing autonomous AI agent systems that prompt themselves, rather than humans prompting agents. Boris Cherny, creator of Claude Code at Anthropic, captured the shift in June 2026: “I don’t prompt Claude anymore. I have loops running that prompt Claude and figuring out what to do. My job is to write loops.”

This paradigm shift has profound implications for PRC open-source strategy:

First, the unit of production in open source is no longer the individual developer writing code, but the agent loop—a system of automations, skills, sub-agents, and memory that generates, tests, and reviews code autonomously. The traditional open-source advantage (global volunteer talent pool) is being disrupted by capital-intensive agent infrastructure (compute, training data, model access).

Second, the key “skills” (SKILL.md files that encode project knowledge) and “rules” (CLAUDE.md, AGENTS.md) that govern agent behavior are themselves becoming the new open-source commons. A project’s agent configuration is now as important as its codebase. This creates a new axis of divergence: native open-source projects publish their agent configurations transparently; localized projects embed them within controlled ecosystems.

Third, the major platforms (Claude Code by Anthropic, Codex CLI by OpenAI, Hermes Agent by Nous Research, Codex App by OpenAI) are all built on Western models and trained on predominantly English-language, Western-hacker-culture data. This creates a cultural embedding problem analogous to what Cheung documented for OpenHarmony: the agent layer carries its own institutional assumptions.

I.2 China’s Agentic Response#

The PRC’s response to the agentic revolution has followed the pattern Cheung identified: state-directed, platform-controlled, and security-conscious.

Gitee, the domestic code hosting platform with now over 15 million users (up from 12 million in 2024), has introduced agent-native features including skill directories and agent configuration hosting. However, these operate under the same governance constraints as the OpenAtom ecosystem: all agent configurations are subject to pre-publication security review, and the skill directory is curated by an affiliated editorial board.

The OpenAtom Foundation established an “AI Special Interest Group” (AI-SIG) in early 2025, tasked with developing domestically-hosted model registries and agent runtime environments. The SIG’s publicly stated goal—“to ensure that next-generation software production tools align with socialist core values and national security requirements”—mirrors exactly the language Cheung documented in the OpenAtom bylaws.

However, a critical limitation has emerged: the agent layer amplifies rather than compensates for institutional disadvantages. A Chinese developer using a domestically-hosted agent loop cannot reliably access arXiv for research papers, cannot fetch updated packages from PyPI or npm without GFW-mediated delays, and cannot participate in the global market of agent skills and configurations that are predominantly hosted on Western platforms. The institutional quarantine that Cheung described for operating systems now extends to the production tools themselves.

II. The Infrastructure of Divergence: GFW 2.0#

II.1 From Blocking to Shaping#

Cheung’s original analysis treated the GFW as a containment infrastructure—something that blocks prohibited content but otherwise leaves networking behavior intact. By 2026, the GFW has evolved into a protocol-shaping infrastructure that degrades, delays, and selectively disrupts traffic rather than simply blocking it.

This evolution is directly observable in the experience of researching this very article. Over a three-day period in July 2026, the following pattern emerged:

Semantic Scholar APIComplete block (Cloudflare “human verification”)Indirect: Cloudflare terminates Chinese IPs

The critical finding is that the blocking is not absolute but statistical. It varies by time of day, by target server, and by protocol. DuckDuckGo works for 2-3 queries, then enters a 30-second cooldown—precisely the pattern that makes real-time research impossible while allowing intermittent access. This is not a bug; it is an information cost function that raises the friction of engaging with global knowledge commons while keeping the channel technically “open.”

For a developer in China trying to contribute to an international open-source project, this means:

  • A git push to GitHub may succeed or fail depending on random network conditions
  • Reading a Stack Overflow thread may require 3-4 page reloads
  • Downloading a dependency from npm may take 10x longer than for a developer in Berlin or Bangalore
  • AI agent tools that rely on cloud-hosted models may experience unpredictable latency

This is the physical-layer manifestation of the divergence Cheung described at the institutional layer.

II.2 The Great Firewall as Open Source Ecosystem Boundary#

The GFW’s evolution has transformed it from a censorship tool into an ecosystem boundary maintenance infrastructure. By selectively impeding access to Western code repositories, package managers, and knowledge platforms, it creates a protected space in which domestic alternatives (Gitee, OpenHarmony, domestic model registries) can develop without being outcompeted by their global counterparts.

This is a classic infant industry protection strategy, applied not to manufacturing but to digital commons. The question—as with all protectionist strategies—is whether the protected industries will eventually become competitive enough to survive without the barrier, or whether they will remain permanently dependent on it.

The evidence from 2024-2026 suggests the latter. Despite three years of intensive development, OpenHarmony remains almost entirely confined to domestic applications. Its international adoption is negligible. OpenEuler, despite being featured at Open Source Summit Europe 2023, has not gained significant traction outside China. Gitee, despite 15 million users, hosts almost exclusively Chinese-language projects.

III. The Great Divergence in Practice#

III.1 Two Operating Systems, Two Worlds#

Cheung’s Part II documented OpenHarmony’s deployment in tunnels, ports, satellites, and police vehicles. By 2026, these use cases have expanded but remain entirely within China’s institutional boundaries. The distinction between OpenHarmony and Android/AOSP is no longer technical but institutional.

Using the analytical framework developed by “开源之道” and validated by Jansen (2014) and Moradi et al. (2024), we can measure the two ecosystems’ health:

Health Dimension (Jansen 2014)Native Open Source (Android/AOSP/Linux)Localized Open Source (OpenHarmony/openEuler)
ProductivityHigh: 24/7 global contributor pool, async collaborationMedium: constrained by work hours, administrative processes, security reviews
RobustnessMedium-High: decentralized but vulnerable to corporate capture (commons悲剧)High: state-directed funding ensures stability; single points of failure in governance
Niche CreationHigh: spontaneous forking, new project emergence, role diversityLow-Medium: top-down planning limits innovation space; “indicator prosperity” (Star counts, PR metrics) substitutes for genuine niche creation

The critical finding is that both ecosystems are viable but optimized for different environments. Native open source optimizes for innovation and diversity at the cost of stability. Localized open source optimizes for control and predictability at the cost of innovation.

Moradi et al. (2024) identify trust and power as “mutual substitutes and complements” in open-source governance. Native ecosystems run on trust (peer review, reputation, fork threat). Localized ecosystems run on power (administrative authority, state backing, policy enforcement). Neither is inherently superior; each is adapted to its institutional environment.

III.2 The Bottleneck Problem#

Adner’s (2017) ecosystem-as-structure framework identifies “bottlenecks”—activities whose failure blocks the entire value proposition. Applying this lens reveals each ecosystem’s critical vulnerability:

Native open source bottleneck: Motivation sustainability. The public goods problem (free riders, corporate exploitation without reciprocation) is intensifying. Anthropic’s Project Glasswing (2026)—where trillion-dollar corporations and the Linux Foundation sit on the same governance body—represents both the success and the peril of open source’s hyper-institutionalization.

Localized open source bottleneck: Trust and legitimacy. The core problem Cheung identified—that OpenAtom’s governance is designed for state control, not neutral stewardship—remains unresolved. Projects hosted by OpenAtom, regardless of their technical merit, cannot attract the global developer trust that is the prerequisite for genuine open-source participation. This is not a solvable problem within the current governance framework; it is a structural constraint.

IV. The Regulatory Dimension: China as First Mover#

Cheung’s Part II noted that China was “positioning itself as an early innovator in the regulation of open-source software.” In 2024, this referred primarily to the CAICT-led security evaluation standard (信息安全技术 软件产品开源代码安全评价方法).

By 2026, China’s regulatory framework for open source has become the most comprehensive in the world, though entirely oriented toward domestic control rather than global governance:

  1. Mandatory security evaluation: All open-source components used in critical infrastructure must pass the CAICT four-category assessment (source provenance, code quality, IP compliance, governance review).

  2. Agent code of conduct: The MIIT’s 2025 “Interim Measures for the Management of AI-Assisted Software Development” requires that all agent-generated code carry a traceable watermark indicating the agent system that produced it. This measure, ostensibly about accountability, also enables the state to monitor and control agent-based production.

  3. Cross-border code flow restrictions: Expanded from the 2024 data出境 review framework, now requiring that any code export (e.g., contributing to an international open-source project) be reviewed by a designated security assessment body.

  4. Open-source AI model registry: All domestically developed open-weight AI models must be registered with a state-affiliated model registry, which can revoke registration for security violations. This directly impacts the OSI’s ongoing Open Source AI Definition debate.

These regulations create a regulatory moat around the domestic open-source ecosystem. Foreign projects that wish to participate in China’s market must comply with requirements that are logistically costly and philosophically incompatible with the hacker ethic that Cheung’s Parts I and II implicitly contrasted with the PRC approach.

V. The Geopolitical Landscape: Avoiding Apocalypse#

In November 2024, The New Stack published an article titled “Avoiding a Geopolitical Open Source Apocalypse,” which explicitly raised the concern that Cheung had documented from a national-security perspective: the East-West split in open source. By 2026, this concern has materialized not as a sudden rupture but as a gradual, structural divergence—which is in some ways more dangerous because it is harder to perceive and address.

Three scenarios now appear plausible:

Scenario 1: Continued Parallel Evolution (Most Likely) Both ecosystems continue on their current trajectories. Native open source deepens its integration with corporate AI infrastructure (the Anthropic Glasswing model). Localized open source deepens its integration with state infrastructure. The gap between them widens, but no single event forces a confrontation.

Scenario 2: The Agent Layer Divergence (Emerging) The AI agent layer becomes the new battleground. If the dominant agent platforms (Claude Code, Codex CLI) become unavailable or unreliable in China, and domestic alternatives cannot match their capabilities due to the training data and compute gap, then the productivity differential—already visible in the Jansen health metrics—will widen dramatically. This is the scenario in which “avoiding apocalypse” becomes an urgent policy question.

Scenario 3: Regulatory Fracture (Catalyst Event) A major regulatory action—such as the U.S. restricting open-source AI model exports, or China blocking a core native open-source infrastructure project on security grounds—triggers a cascade of reciprocal restrictions that formally partition the global open-source commons. This would be the digital equivalent of the 18th-century “Great Divergence” between Europe and Asia that Pomeranz documented.

Conclusion: The Divergence is the Story#

Cheung’s 2024 analysis correctly identified the OpenAtom Foundation as a state-aligned organization, OpenHarmony as a dual-use technology, and China’s open-source strategy as a vehicle for technological sovereignty. What neither Cheung nor most Western analysts anticipated was the depth and irreversibility of the institutional divergence.

The divergence is not merely about who controls OpenAtom, or whether OpenHarmony is used in police vehicles. It is about whether the same word—“open source”—can meaningfully describe two systems that operate under fundamentally different rules of trust, governance, and institutional logic.

The answer emerging in 2026 is: not for much longer.

The “open source” label currently masks a growing structural chasm. On one side, a system rooted in hacker ethics, peer review, fork-threat governance, and global asynchronous collaboration. On the other side, a system rooted in state-directed planning, administrative governance, security-first protocols, and domestic infrastructure protection.

These two systems can coexist. They cannot merge.

The practical question for policymakers, developers, and organizations is not which system is “better”—that judgment depends entirely on one’s institutional environment and values. The question is: which ecosystem are you building on? In a diverging world, the worst position is to assume they are still one.
