China Open Source Daily — 2026-11-18

Two documents from the fortnight, read against each other, say the same thing from opposite ends of the supply chain. openEuler closed its summit on 2026-11-14 reporting that cumulative domestic installations of openEuler-family operating systems will pass 22 million and that Frost & Sullivan expects it to take 61 per cent of China's new server-OS market in 2026, on 2,200-plus member organisations and 30,000-plus contributors, renaming its own category from server OS to Agentic Infra OS and setting two internal targets stated in the same breath: 80 per cent of CVEs handled with AI assistance by year-end, and 100 per cent of the community's service agents onboarded. The same fortnight, China Industrial News on 2026-11-16 ran the industry's own attempt to name the economics of giving away models — Huang Tie-jun's BAAI deputy head Ye Qiwei saying that open versus closed is an economic choice and that open source has mostly been the follower's posture, because occupying an ecological niche is what a laggard needs; Chen Bohui of the Asia Digital Economy Academy adding that a downloaded model is only step one, since running, evaluating and maintaining it all cost money, and if those capabilities remain concentrated in a few cloud platforms then profit keeps flowing to the compute provider even after the model is open. Meanwhile Zhejiang's seven-department open-source implementation opinion, in force since 2026-07-20, writes the fix into the rulebook in one clause: open-source contributions enter professional-title assessment for university teachers, talent assessment and enterprise staff appraisal — while the funding instruments it relies on are token vouchers, compute vouchers and data vouchers, and its 2027 targets are counted in projects, enterprises and demonstration sites. Installations are the metric that grows fastest and proves least; contributions are the metric that no instrument pays for. Both documents are about the same gap.

⚠️ Editorial note: The open source ecosystem in China operates under a distinct institutional framework — characterized by state-led initiatives, intranet-like boundaries, and top-down governance. Readers should be aware that this context differs from the community-driven open source model common in other regions. The term “open source” as used in Chinese media may refer to practices that diverge from the conventional definition.

China Open Source Daily — 2026-11-18#

🏗️ Institutional Change — Code as a Line Item: How the Industry’s Own Editors Justified Giving Models Away, and What They Say It Costs to Run Them#

1. The document, and why it is the one to read. On 2026-11-16, China Industrial News (中国工业报) — the newspaper of the China Federation of Industrial Economics, i.e. an industry paper, not a university or a ministry — published a feature by reporter Geng Pengfei (耿鹏飞), syndicated the same day on Sohu, titled “Open source ‘accelerating’: Chinese large models race the global intelligence track” (开源"加速跑" 中国大模型竞逐全球智能赛道). What makes it quotable is not its statistics, which are the standard round of the season, but the two named analysts it allows to say what the round of statistics is actually about. The piece was published the day before DeepSeek imposed its first free-tier caps (examined in this series’ 2026-11-17 entry, on a report dated 2026-11-16), which is the reason it is worth reading twice rather than once.

2. The numbers it reports, stated as it reports them. Alibaba’s Tsai Chung-hsin (蔡崇信) told the Wuzhen summit on 2026-11-03 that Qwen has open-sourced more than 470 models over three years, with over 4 billion cumulative global downloads and more than 330,000 derivative models, first among open model families. Tencent released Hunyuan Hy4 on 2026-11-04, its first open model with visual capability, which the paper says is the first time Hunyuan has “joined the ranks of the strongest open models.” Moonshot open-sourced Kimi K3 at 2.8 trillion total parameters at the end of July, the largest open model in the world by parameter count; DeepSeek’s V4 series put a million-character context window into the open domain; Qwen continued a full-size, full-modality line. On the survey side: Hugging Face reports 41 per cent of its platform downloads over the past year were of Chinese-developed open models, a measure that excludes private deployments and domestic platforms including ModelScope; domestic cumulative downloads have passed 10 billion; and per CAICT’s Large Model Token Service Performance Monitoring Report (2026), across 22 major domestic and foreign platforms surveyed, DeepSeek, Qwen and the GLM series each had a 100 per cent onboarding rate — the Chinese open models are the base every platform carries.

3. The two sentences the paper allows. Ye Qiwei (叶启威), vice-president of BAAI (北京智源人工智能研究院), is quoted saying: “the choice between open and closed is less a dispute over technical routes than an economic choice. The choice to open source is customer-first — helping downstream users explore how AI lands; closed source pays more attention to AI’s own growth. The deeper reason is that open-source models have mostly been in the posture of a follower, and therefore need open source to occupy an ecological niche.” And then, in a sentence that is doing more work than its length suggests: “once an open model catches up with or even leads in some dimension, the nature of open source quietly changes from a tool for catching up into a tool for setting standards.” Chen Bohui (陈柏珲), dean of the Asia Digital Economy Academy, supplies the second: citing Indonesian GoTo and AI Singapore having built an Indonesian/Javanese/Sundanese-language variant of SEA-LION as Sahabat-AI, he says “whoever is familiar with how local people speak has the chance to participate in model development and decide whom it should serve and which problems it should solve”; and, on the cost side, that downloading a model is only the first step — running, evaluating and maintaining it all cost money, and if those capabilities remain concentrated in a few cloud platforms, then even with the model open, profit may keep concentrating in the compute provider. His portability test for industrial autonomy is worth quoting entire: “if the supplier raises prices tomorrow or stops service, can the enterprise take its own business data and switch to another model and keep operating?” — with the recommendation that government and large enterprises write data export and service-continuity periods into procurement contracts, and for critical systems actually switch models once before go-live, to see what it costs and how long it is interrupted.

4. The finding buried in the paper’s own framing. The article’s third section is titled “open source enters deep water” and lists three unsolved problems: homogenisation (everyone on Transformer, general capability gaps narrowing, leadership rotating), monetisation (“open source brings broader ecological influence but inherently has the pain point of more investment and little or no return; in the short term it is hard to bring profit directly”), and security — the last illustrated with a concrete regulatory fact: after the open agent application OpenClaw went viral, MIIT’s National Vulnerability Database (NVDB) and CNCERT both issued risk advisories about high-severity vulnerabilities and derivative risks in it. The paper’s two named remedies, from Wang Huan (王桓) of the Chinese Institute of Electronics and Wei Kai (魏凯) of CAICT’s AI institute, are a whole-process open-source technology security review mechanism with named responsible parties, and deepening open-source governance and standardisation, led by industry standards on data-set compliance and model evaluation, “actively integrating into the international open-source governance system” and converting China’s practice into international rules.

Institutional Analysis & Commentary:

A gift that costs money to run is not a gift; it is an asset with a liability attached, and this fortnight the industry’s own newspaper said so in plain language from both sides of the ledger.

First, Ye Qiwei’s formulation is the cleanest statement in Chinese industry print of what this series has been calling an ecological position rather than a commons. “The deeper reason is that open-source models have mostly been in the posture of a follower, and therefore need open source to occupy an ecological niche.” Unpack it: a follower’s cheapest asset is other people’s adoption. A leader defends a moat; a follower tries to become the default that nobody has to argue about, and the way to become the default is to make the alternative free. This is a textbook account of openness as a competitive strategy under conditions of asymmetric capability — Coase’s insight inverted: where transaction costs are high and brand is weak, a firm lowers the cost of transacting with itself by giving the interface away and capturing value later. It is not cynicism and it is not hypocrisy. It is a statement of the strategic logic that makes a commons — and of the moment at which a commons stops being strategically necessary.

Second, and this is the sentence this entry exists for: “once an open model catches up with or even leads in some dimension, the nature of open source quietly changes from a tool for catching up into a tool for setting standards.” Read it as a prediction with a trigger condition, and note that the trigger is being pulled elsewhere in the same document: 41 per cent of Hugging Face downloads, 4 billion Qwen downloads, 330,000 derivatives, 100 per cent onboarding across 22 platforms. If adoption is the follower’s instrument, then the moment the follower is being adopted by others, the strategic reason to keep giving it away has inverted — and the incentive moves to the interface, the harness, the standard and the metering. DeepSeek’s 40,000-character ceiling, published the day after this article, is the first hard evidence that the inversion is already happening at the consumer terminal. Chen Bohui’s cost argument and DeepSeek’s meter are the same argument arriving from two directions: whoever owns the running, evaluating and maintaining owns the profit, and a downloaded artefact confers no right to any of those.

Third, the portability test deserves to be treated as an institution and not a tip. “If the supplier raises prices tomorrow or stops service, can the enterprise take its own business data and switch to another model and keep operating?” — and the recommendation to write data export and service-continuity periods into procurement contracts, and to switch once before go-live to find out the real cost. That is a contract-based exit right, which is precisely the institution that open licences do not create. A permissive licence guarantees you may copy the model; it guarantees nothing about your ability to migrate your data, your evaluation set, your fine-tunes or your pipelines away from the platform that serves it. The thing that makes a supplier competitive is not the licence on the weights but the credible threat of exit — and exit is created by contract terms, data-portability rules and practised migrations, none of which any licence grants. Chinese procurement, which is where the state’s leverage actually sits, would be the natural place to standardise that clause. As of this document, nobody has.

Fourth, the security section is more institutionally interesting than the monetisation section, and it is the one the paper buries. MIIT’s NVDB and CNCERT issuing risks advisories on a popular open agent application is a regulatory fact with a governance consequence: the state has now established that it can name an open project as a risk to the public without being its maintainer, its funder or its contributor. That is a monitoring and early-warning function, not a liability regime — the advisory names the risk and does not name who is responsible for the fix, which is exactly why Wang Huan’s recommendation is to “clarify the responsible subject” (明确安全责任主体). Note what that proposal does to the ecosystem’s incentive geometry: if an individual maintainer of a widely-used open project can be designated the responsible subject for its vulnerabilities, maintenance has gone from an unpriced externality to an unbounded personal liability, and the rational response of every competent maintainer is to stop publishing. The alternative institution — a fund that indemnifies maintainers, or a legal entity that carries the duty and has revenue — does not appear in the article, and it is the missing half of the sentence.

Fifth, the falsifiable tests. If the “standard-setting tool” transition is real, within four quarters at least one major Chinese open-weight lab will publish a licence that carries a service or governance term rather than pure permission (a trademark clause with teeth, a hosted-service restriction, a contribution-licence requirement), and this series will be able to date the inversion. If Chen Bohui’s cost thesis holds, the pricing power will show up in cloud inference margins rather than in model pricing — watch whether any major domestic cloud publishes a per-token price for a Chinese open model that is above the cost of self-hosting, and whether self-hosting documentation improves or degrades. And on the security question: if an open-source security responsibility regime is created in China in the next twelve months, does it create an entity that can be held liable, or a person? A regime that holds entities liable will make foundations stronger; a regime that holds persons liable will make them empty.

Checkable questions.

(i) Does any Chinese open-weight lab publish, alongside its next model, a documented migration path — weights plus data-export schema plus an evaluation set — that a third party can use to leave its hosted platform? Chen Bohui’s test is only meaningful if someone runs it. A lab that publishes the exit route is signalling confidence; a lab that does not is pricing lock-in.

(ii) Will MIIT’s and CNCERT’s advisories on open agent software ever name a subject responsible for remediation — and if so, will it be the publisher, the distributor or the maintainer? The answer determines whether China builds a product-liability regime for open source or a personal-liability one, and those two regimes produce opposite supply curves for volunteers.

(iii) Does the standard-setting turn arrive as a standard or as a licence change? A standard adopted through a national technical committee is a consensus instrument that others can join; a licence change is a unilateral one. Ye Qiwei’s sentence leaves both open, and the difference is the whole difference between a commons with rules and a platform with terms.

🏛️ Policy & Regulation — The Ceiling That Was Never a Floor: Zhejiang’s Seven-Department Opinion, and the One Clause That Is Not a Subsidy#

1. The document. On 2026-07-20, with the approval of the Zhejiang Provincial People’s Government, the Zhejiang Provincial Department of Economy and Information Technology and six other departments (7部门) issued and brought into force the “Implementation Opinion on Accelerating the Construction of Zhejiang Province’s Open-Source System” (《关于加快推进浙江省开源体系建设的实施意见》). It is one of the two provincial-level dedicated open-source system documents the country has produced — Beijing and Shanghai published theirs earlier in the year — and a July commentary on the OpenAtom Foundation’s site called 2025 the year China’s local open-source development “crossed a watershed,” counting more than 70 local policies containing explicit open-source content nationwide. The opinion has not appeared in this series before; it is examined here because of one clause in its last section, and because the rest of the document shows what that clause is up against.

2. What it commits to, by 2027, in numbers. One to two open-source communities with global influence; three to four nationally known ones; two or more high-level open-source research institutions; 50-plus high-quality open-source projects; 150-plus open-source innovation enterprises; 100-plus open-source application benchmarks — of which 40-plus in manufacturing, 30-plus in services, and 30-plus on OpenHarmony. Six workstreams: infrastructure, industrial innovation led by open source, AI open-source innovation, application integration, entity cultivation, and an open and flourishing ecosystem. The instruments: support for an open-source sector alliance; universities, research institutes and leading firms to build research institutions and communities in AI, humanoid robotics, intelligent driving, industrial software and chip design; high-quality data sets in industry, transport, health, education and science, with public data opened to open-source communities as “core fuel”; AI model supply expanded in vertical domains — visual intelligence, industrial manufacturing, smart healthcare, smart finance, e-commerce retail, game and cultural creativity, biomedicine; 40-plus manufacturing benchmarks under the “415X” advanced manufacturing cluster programme; and, for internationalisation, channels to international open-source organisations and industry alliances and support for international-grade open-source organisations to establish themselves in Zhejiang.

3. The clause that is not a subsidy. In the section on talent and culture, the opinion states that open-source contributions enter professional-title assessment and appointment for university teachers, talent-title assessment, and enterprise staff performance appraisal as important indicators (将开源贡献纳入高校教师职称评聘、人才职称评定、企业职工考核的重要指标). Read that against every instrument in the rest of the document. The state can pay a firm for a project, a district for a platform, a university for a course. It cannot pay a person for a review. What it can do is make the review count as a qualification — which is a different instrument entirely, operating on the credential market rather than the fiscal one, and it is the only mechanism in the document that could plausibly reward maintenance work rather than artefact delivery. This series has been asking since October whether any Chinese instrument pays an individual for contribution rather than an organisation for a project. Zhejiang did not answer that with money. It answered it with a recognition claim on a license to teach — the one currency a professional in a state-legitimated system cannot refuse.

4. What the rest of the document reveals about the constraint. The opinion’s money-side instruments are named exactly: “explore dedicated open-source system policies,” encourage local governments to make coordinated use of Token vouchers (Token券), compute vouchers (算力券) and data vouchers (数据券), and give policy support to platforms established by international organisations that land locally. The unit of subsidy is consumption. A compute voucher buys inference hours; a data voucher buys access to a data set; a token voucher buys model calls. Not one of them buys a maintainer’s afternoon, because none of them can be denominated in a deliverable that a finance office will accept. The targets, likewise, are counts of things that can be enumerated by an administrative body at year end: communities, institutions, projects, enterprises, benchmarks. And the AI emphasis is on supply — more models, more vertical models, more open tooling — which is precisely the side of the market that the industry’s own editors were, four months later, describing as saturated and hard to monetise.

Institutional Analysis & Commentary:

This document is the clearest specimen in the series of a policy that contains its own correction and cannot fund it.

First, separate the two halves of the opinion by what they actually do. The fiscal half (vouchers plus countable targets) is an adoption subsidy: it lowers the price of consuming open artefacts, denominated in units of consumption, audited by counts of consumers and deployments. The credential half (contributions count in title assessment) is a provision instrument: it lowers the opportunity cost of doing unpaid maintenance, and it does so by attaching the work to a status that the system already has a register for. These are not two intensities of the same policy. They are two different theories of why anyone would contribute — one says people contribute because it is cheap to use the output, the other says people contribute because the work is counted where it counts. The first produces installed bases. The second produces maintainers. Zhejiang has written both into one document and has only ever built the machinery for the first.

Second, and this is the point that matters for every province: a credential is an institution only if the assessor can verify the claim. “Open-source contribution is an important indicator in professional-title assessment” is a delegation without a verification standard. The moment a university’s personnel office has to decide whether a candidate’s contribution is worth a title, it needs a metric — and the metrics available are the ones this series has documented all year: commit counts, project star counts, competition prizes, platform score points. Each is gameable in a different way, and none requires a merged patch reviewed by a stranger who does not work for your employer, which is the only contribution-shaped event that a commons actually needs. So the clause will be implemented, and it will be implemented with the instruments that already exist — which is why the observatory’s 2026-11-03 finding and the Zhejiang clause fit together: the Chinese state publishes caps and rates and rules because the rule is what the centre actually decided, and here the centre has decided that open source counts for a title without deciding what counts as open source.

Third, the AI section reveals which side of the market the province believes is scarce, and the answer is wrong in an instructive way. “Increase AI model open supply, focusing on visual intelligence, industrial manufacturing, smart healthcare, smart finance, e-commerce retail, game and cultural creativity, biomedicine — make a batch of vertical specialised models excellent and strong, support model projects to be open-sourced.” More models is a supply subsidy into a market where the industry’s own analysts, four months later, report homogenisation, rotation of leadership, and a monetisation path that “has not been fully worked out.” The scarce thing in November 2026 is not models. It is the running, evaluating and maintaining that Chen Bohui described — and there is no voucher for that, because a voucher for maintenance would require an invoice from a person for an absence of incidents. A province can subsidise the artefact; it cannot subsidise the relationship. The opinion is honest enough to say the relationship matters, and structured so that only the artefact can be bought.

Fourth, note what the opinion does not do, and it is the same omission as every document in this series. No audit line for awards. No named-individual grant. No SLA or exit terms written into public procurement (which is exactly Chen Bohui’s proposal, and Zhejiang is one of China’s largest public-sector IT buyers). No requirement that a subsidised open project accept external contributions on published terms. What it does instead is internationalise the entry — “support international-grade open-source organisations to establish themselves in Zhejiang” — which is a land-and-expand instrument aimed at foundations, not at contributors. The province is bidding for the headquarters of the commons. The commons is made in the afternoon of a maintainer in another jurisdiction.

Fifth, the falsifiable test, and Zhejiang will run it for us within eighteen months. If, by the 2027 target date, at least one Zhejiang university’s professional-title assessment publishes an explicit, verifiable contribution standard — a named repository, a merge threshold, an external reviewer requirement — then the credential instrument has been built, and this series’ “no instrument reaches the maintenance layer” finding will have to be revised. If instead the clause degrades into “participated in an open-source competition” and “obtained an open-source capability certificate,” then the regime has converted a provision problem into a training problem, and the OPC nine-level ladder documented on 2026-11-16 will have swallowed the only clause that pointed elsewhere. Both are observable, and the difference will be printed in a personnel-office document that nobody outside China will read.

Checkable questions.

(i) Which of the seven issuing departments owns the contribution-recognition clause, and what verification standard has it issued? A joint opinion with seven signatories usually means one drafter and six consents. The drafter’s implementing rules will show whether the clause has teeth.

(ii) Do the Token, compute and data vouchers have a published redemption rate and a named recipient list? Zhejiang is one of the more transparent provinces; the 2026-11-03 comparison noted that Chinese support is visible as rules and ceilings, with award notices scattered. A voucher programme’s redemption data would be the first real disbursement-level evidence this series has asked for since 2026-11-10.

(iii) Does any Zhejiang public procurement notice — provincial or municipal — include data-export and service-continuity terms for an AI or operating-system contract? Chen Bohui proposed exactly this on 2026-11-16. If a province that has written an open-source opinion does not write exit terms into its own contracts, then exit rights are not part of the Chinese theory of open source at all.

📊 The Ledger — A Node That Has Become a Pipe: Twenty-Two Million Installations, and the Number Nobody in the Announcement Reports#

1. The quantity, and its provenance. Across 2026-11-13 and 14, at the Agentic Infra Operating System Conference and openEuler Summit 2026 in Beijing’s Zhongguancun International Innovation Centre, the openEuler community reported that cumulative domestic installations of openEuler-family operating systems are expected to pass 22 million, with Frost & Sullivan (沙利文) forecasting a 61 per cent share of China’s new server-OS market in 2026; 2,200-plus member organisations and more than 30,000 open-source contributors; coverage of finance, telecoms, energy and other key sectors; and entry into satellite systems and embodied intelligence. The baseline is stated in the same reports: in November 2024 openEuler reported 10 million cumulative installations in five years of open source, of which more than 5 million were added in 2024 alone — so the 22 million figure is a doubling in roughly two years, at a compound rate of about 48 per cent a year. OpenAtom Foundation chairman Xie Shaofeng (谢少锋) disclosed the number; openEuler committee chair Xiong Wei (熊伟) released 24.03 LTS SP5; Huawei ICT strategy chief Peng Honghua (彭红华) spoke as “strategic donor and core contributor.”

2. What “upgraded” actually means in this announcement. openEuler announced it is now an operating system for Agentic Infra — the technical substrate for building, running, connecting, monitoring and managing AI agents — with sandboxes, KV cache, memory systems and agent security as named capabilities, already in commercial deployment with leading users in internet and finance. The community plans, by year-end, 80 per cent of CVEs handled with AI assistance and 100 per cent of community service agents onboarded. Xie’s two proposals are the institutional content: first, hold the public base firmer and build it higher — from code co-building to standards co-building to governance co-building, making openEuler the industry’s common base; second, fight a joint battle in the Agentic Infra lane. Huawei’s three commitments: continued AI capability contribution (sandboxes, memory, agent security governance), increased investment in community infrastructure including the SkillHub tool set to make community development and service agentic, and joint go-to-market with partners.

3. The number that is not in the announcement. Nowhere in the summit coverage, the foundation chairman’s remarks or the vendor’s commitments is there a figure for upstream contributions from openEuler to the projects it is built on — no kernel patch-merge count, no share of fixes returned to upstream maintainers, no count of CVEs fixed in the distribution and fixed upstream. This is not an accusation of concealment; such a figure is simply not among the metrics anyone reports, which is itself the finding. The distribution measures what it delivers to its install base and what it absorbs from the commons; the return path is unmeasured on both sides. This series documented on 2026-11-05 that openEuler’s canonical kernel repository had moved from Gitee to AtomGit while a GitHub mirror named openeuler-mirror sat 1,245,880 commits behind with zero issues and zero pull requests — meaning that the only mechanically queryable surface of the project is a one-way mirror. A project whose inbound channel is invisible from outside cannot be assessed from outside on the one dimension that matters most to a commons.

4. The other figure worth pausing on, because it is the most interesting thing in the announcement. “80 per cent of CVEs handled with AI assistance by year-end.” A CVE-handling rate is an operational KPI — it measures how fast a known defect is triaged, patched and shipped to users. It is exactly the kind of target that a distribution with a large install base and a vendor-funded engineering staff is good at, and it is the kind of target that a volunteer upstream is not. The asymmetry is structural and it is not a criticism: a downstream distributor with 22 million installations has every reason to be fast at CVE response, and being fast at CVE response is a genuine public good. But note where the incentive lands. A downstream that fixes fast has less reason to upstream the fix, because the fix’s value to its own install base is realised the moment it ships in its own repository. The value of upstreaming accrues to other distributions and to future maintainers — which is to say, to parties who are not in the room and are not counted in any of the numbers above.

Institutional Analysis & Commentary:

Twenty-two million installations is a real achievement and it is not the achievement the announcement thinks it is. It measures the success of a distribution, and the question a commons must answer is about a node.

First, put the two numbers side by side and the shape of the thing becomes visible. 22 million installed units is a stock measure of adoption. 30,000 contributors is a stock measure of participation, and it is not the same population as the ones counted in any code metric — this series noted on 2026-11-08 that Zhejiang University’s Taiyi 2.0 platform exists precisely to aggregate commits, issues, merge requests and human-AI collaboration into a verifiable score, which is an acknowledgment that the participant count and the contribution count are not the same number and that the ecosystem needs a machine to tell them apart. 61 per cent of the new server-OS market is a market-share figure with a named source (Frost & Sullivan), which means it is a purchased estimate reported as a fact in an official-setting speech — a practice this series has flagged repeatedly and should flag again: it is not fraud, it is the substitution of a consultancy’s model for an audited count, and in a market where the state is the dominant buyer, a forecast share becomes a self-fulfilling procurement signal. Three numbers, three different epistemologies, one slide.

Second, the rename is the institutional event and it is easy to miss. “openEuler upgrades to an operating system for Agentic Infra” is not a version bump. It is a reclassification of the project’s category, and category matters because procurement, subsidy eligibility, standards working groups and talent curricula are all organised by category. This series documented on 2026-11-15 that MIIT’s Industrial Operating System Standards Working Group had been constituted; a community that renames itself into “Agentic Infra” is positioning to be the reference implementation of that category before the category’s standards are drafted. This is the most effective form of institutional influence available to a project in this system: not lobbying a rule, but becoming the thing the rule is written about. And it is legitimate — it is what the Linux Foundation, CNCF and every standards-setting open project does. The difference is who the category’s buyer is: in Europe a category attracts vendors, in China a category attracts procurement.

Third, the “governance co-building” proposal is where Xie’s remarks get interesting, and where they get thin. “From code co-building to standards co-building to governance co-building, making openEuler the industry’s common base.” Governance co-building is the right ask, and it is the only one of the three that would change anything structural. But the announcement does not say what governance change is proposed, who would hold what vote, or how a new member organisation acquires standing in the technical committee. The summit’s published speaker roster is instructive here: it is dense with SIG maintainers and committers from Huawei, Kylinsoft, China Mobile Cloud, Inspur, SoftStone, ISCAS, XJTLU, Beihang, Shanghai Jiao Tong, Intel, Linaro — a genuinely broad technical bench, and a governance bench that is not described anywhere. Co-building code is evidenced by the speaker list; co-building governance is evidenced by a charter amendment, and there is none in the material.

Fourth, the honest caveat, and it is the one that keeps this entry from being an attack. openEuler is, by any comparative standard, one of the most substantial open-source projects outside North America and Europe: 22 million installations, a real technical community, a genuine technical agenda (the super-node OS work documented in this series’ 2026-11-14 entry was released before the model vendors were talking about agent sandboxes), and a foundation graduation in November 2025 that few Chinese projects have achieved. It is a downstream of the Linux kernel written by people who maintain a real codebase and ship real fixes to real users, and that is more than most funded open-source projects in any country accomplish. The finding of this entry is not that it is hollow. It is that the measurements chosen to describe it — installations, market share, member organisations, contributors, CVE response rate — are all measures of a node on a network, and there is no measure anywhere in the announcement of whether the node gives more than it takes. In a commons, that is the only question. In a distribution, it never comes up.

Fifth, the falsifiable tests, and they are unusually easy for a public project. Does openEuler publish, in any of its monthly community reports, a count of patches submitted upstream to the Linux kernel, systemd, glibc or the other projects it consumes? This series documented the format on 2026-11-05: the community publishes monthly operational reports. A single line would settle the question. Does the Agentic Infra category produce a specification that other distributions can implement and be certified against, or a set of APIs that only openEuler provides? A category with a spec is an institution; a category with an API is a product rename. And the cheapest tell: when a CVE is fixed in openEuler’s tree, does the advisory say whether the fix came from upstream, went upstream, or neither? Three words in an advisory template, and the return path becomes measurable — which is why no one has added them.

Checkable questions.

(i) Does the 22 million figure count installations or units, and is the 61 per cent a shipped-seat share or a revenue share? The two are not interchangeable, and a procurement-relevant share should be reconcilable against the buyers’ own disclosures. OpenEuler’s covered sectors — finance, telecoms, energy — are sectors whose IT spending is partially disclosed in filings.

(ii) What does “100 per cent of community service agents onboarded” mean for a human contributor’s standing? If service agents become the interface through which new contributors are triaged, the community has, in effect, appointed an automated reviewer. That is defensible; it is also the moment at which the answer to “who reviews the patch” stops being a person, and the governance charter should say so.

(iii) Does the Agentic Infra operating system ship its agent sandbox, memory system and KV cache as separable upstreamable components, or as distribution features? The architecture choice determines whether the work can travel. A component can go upstream; a feature tied to a distribution’s catalogue cannot, and 22 million installations will not change that.

🔍 Commentary — The Cost Per Installation Is Falling, and the Cost Per Review Is Not#

First, this morning’s three documents are one document read at three distances. Zhejiang’s opinion writes a credential clause into a voucher-based subsidy system and cannot fund the half that would matter. China Industrial News prints the industry’s own analysts saying, in effect, that the model is cheap and the running of it is not, and proposing exit rights written into procurement contracts as the remedy — a contractual institution that Chinese open-source policy has never mentioned. openEuler reports 22 million installations and 61 per cent of new server-OS share and states, as its technical agenda, that most of its CVE work will soon be done by AI. Read together: the artifact has never been cheaper, the running of it is where the money goes, and the work of maintaining the commons is the one line item no voucher, no ceiling and no installation count touches.

Second, the fortnight’s real discovery is a sentence from BAAI, not a policy: “once an open model catches up or leads, the nature of open source quietly changes from a tool for catching up into a tool for setting standards.” This is the best available statement of why the Chinese open-source surge will not simply continue as an open-source surge — and the proof arrived one day later, when DeepSeek put a 40,000-character ceiling on its free terminal. A follower opens to occupy a niche. A leader opens to set a standard, and standards are enforced through interfaces, licences and default placement rather than through source availability. Watch for that shift in licences, not in announcements: the next Chinese open-weight release that carries a service term rather than pure permission will be the date the second phase began.

Third, and this is the uncomfortable symmetry the series has to record. Both systems in this comparison are failing the same test from opposite directions. Europe buys maintenance in small amounts (curl, OpenSSL, KDE) and does not buy adoption; China buys adoption at scale (22 million installations, vouchers, benchmarks) and does not buy maintenance. The European failure is that its maintenance funding is too small to sustain anything but a handful of critical utilities; the Chinese failure is that its provision is entirely absent because no finance office can accept an invoice for an incident that did not happen. Neither system pays for the afternoon that makes either one work. The difference is that Europe at least has the instrument and under-funds it, while China has written the sentence and not the instrument — and sentences are easier to publish than funds to appropriate.

Fourth, the honest caveat. It is tempting to read the credential clause as a breakthrough. It is a beginning, and it has a failure mode that this series has already documented. On 2026-11-16 the briefing noted the OPC nine-level capability ladder from Hangzhou’s AI open-source ecosystem college, and asked whether any level could be attained by coursework alone. The Zhejiang clause has the same vulnerability from the other end: any contribution standard that a personnel office can administer will be built from the metrics it already administers — competitions, certificates, project deliverables, counts. The one contribution that a commons needs, a merged patch reviewed by a stranger, has no certificate and no count, and the probability that a provincial personnel system invents that verifier before it imports the ones it already has is not zero, but it is not high. The clause should be judged in 2028 by the text of a Zhejiang university’s implementation rule, not by the opinion’s sentence.

Fifth, what to watch, concretely. Zhejiang’s voucher redemption data — the first Chinese disbursement-level record this series has asked for, and one province is capable of publishing it. openEuler’s monthly community reports, for a single line on patches sent upstream; the reports exist, and the line is absent, which is itself a finding that will keep being a finding until someone adds it. And the exit clause question in Chinese public procurement — if a province that has just written an open-source implementation opinion signs AI contracts with no data-export or service-continuity terms, then the Chinese theory of open source has no exit right in it, and the open licence is doing only the work it was designed to do: making the copy free and the switching cost invisible.
