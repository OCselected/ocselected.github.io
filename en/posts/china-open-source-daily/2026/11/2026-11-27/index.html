The foundation's 2025 audited statements are now public, and this briefing read them on the page images rather than from a summary. What they show: donation income of CNY 128.84 million in 2025, up from CNY 115.75 million in 2024, received from 108 corporate donors against 47 the year before. Total income CNY 145.69 million; total expenditure CNY 136.71 million, of which CNY 129.16 million was classified as charitable activity; administration CNY 7.55 million, or 5.52 per cent. Government subsidy income in 2025 was CNY 4.88 million — down from CNY 20.7 million in 2023. Net assets at year end CNY 171.36 million, up from CNY 162.19 million. Every figure here is a fact about money and none of them is a fact about code. That is the entire institutional content of the disclosure regime: a foundation that files its donations to the yuan and does not file a single contribution count. Two things happened this week that matter more than the numbers. The filings became legible to outsiders through the OCR work of a Europe-based research observatory, not through the foundation's own publishing practice — the disclosure existed, the readability did not. And a comparison chapter published by the same observatory closed with the finding that China publishes per-project caps and rates while Europe publishes budgets and disbursements, so that any headline comparison in either direction is today unfounded. The asymmetry is not in the money. It is in who can be read.

⚠️ Editorial note: The open source ecosystem in China operates under a distinct institutional framework — characterized by state-led initiatives, intranet-like boundaries, and top-down governance. Readers should be aware that this context differs from the community-driven open source model common in other regions. The term “open source” used in Chinese media may refer to practices that diverge from the conventional definition.

China Open Source Daily — 2026-11-27#

🏛️ Governance — A Hundred and Eight Cheques: Reading OpenAtom’s 2025 Audited Statements#

1. What the document is, and where it lives. The OpenAtom Open Source Foundation (开放原子开源基金会) publishes its financial disclosure on a page separate from the annual work reports: openatom.cn/information/audit (审计报告), which carries, for each year from 2020 to 2025, both a full annual audit report (年度审计报告) and a special-information audit report (专项信息审核报告)1. The 2025 pair was uploaded 2026-08-19; the 2020–2022 pairs were uploaded 2026-11-24, five days ago. It is this page — not the more obvious /information/work page, which holds only scanned annual work reports and stops at 2023 — that carries the actual statements, and the distinction matters because a researcher who checked the wrong page in early November concluded correctly that no 2024 or 2025 report was published, and had to correct themselves a week later2.

2. The 2025 income statement, read from the page image. The table below is transcribed from the 2025 annual audit report, page carrying the 业务活动表 (statement of activities). The report was audited by 中审华会计师事务所（特殊普通合伙）北京分所 and signed 2026-03-011:

  • Donation income: CNY 128,840,000 — against CNY 115,745,500 in 2024, which is the “previous year” column of the same statement.
  • Service income: CNY 9,920,078.99; other income: CNY 2,044,373.89.
  • Government subsidy income: CNY 4,882,853.23 — against nil in 2024.
  • Total income: CNY 145,687,306.11.
  • Charitable activity cost (业务活动成本): CNY 129,164,418.43.
  • Administration (管理费用): CNY 7,548,217.95, which is 5.52 per cent of total expenditure.
  • Total expenditure: CNY 136,713,958.83.
  • Net assets at year end: CNY 171,357,919.55, of which CNY 119.47 million unrestricted and CNY 42.71 million restricted — against CNY 162.19 million at end-2024.

The cash-flow statement in the same report gives the collection side: cash received in donations CNY 149,057,000 in 2025 against CNY 105,890,000 in 2024; net cash from operating activity CNY 42.66 million; cash paid for fixed and intangible assets CNY 18.30 million, against CNY 1.31 million the year before — a fourteen-fold step in capital expenditure, which is the one line in the whole document that describes something other than a stream of money.

3. The donor count, which is the number that carries the institutional argument. The 2025 special-information audit report records the donations as coming from 108 corporate donors, up from 47 in 20242. That is a 2.3-fold increase in the number of paying organisations in one year, and it is the most consequential number in today’s briefing, because it describes a change in the shape of the funding base rather than its size — total donations rose 11 per cent while the number of donors rose 130 per cent. The same report gives the large-donor schedule, and the composition is worth reading against the 2023 baseline this series has already covered:

  • Huawei-affiliated entities gave CNY 27.85 million in 2023, 37 per cent of all donations. In 2025 the named Huawei entities in the large-donor schedule total roughly CNY 8 million, unless an CNY 18.95 million first line whose donor name is illegible in the OCR turns out to be one of them2.
  • The 2024 top donors include Huawei Petal Cloud (CNY 14.0M), Huawei Terminal (CNY 8.34M), Shenzhen Kaihong (CNY 7.5M), Kylin Software (CNY 7.0M), UnionTech (CNY 6.2M) — and Intel Asia-Pacific R&D (CNY 6.0M)2.
  • The 2025 schedule adds ChinaSoft International (CNY 8.0M), Hygon (CNY 6.2M), Hunan Kylinsec (CNY 6.0M), China Southern Power Grid (CNY 5.0M), iSoftStone (CNY 4.0M), Alibaba Cloud (CNY 3.0M), Xiaomi (CNY 3.0M), Inspur Research (CNY 3.0M) — and two county-level and prefecture-level city construction-investment groups at CNY 3.0 million each2.

4. The three findings the numbers support, stated as narrowly as the evidence allows. First, the government subsidy channel is now small relative to the donation channel: CNY 4.88 million in 2025 against CNY 20.7 million in 2023, of which CNY 20.2 million had been restricted to an ecosystem-cultivation platform. If a reader wanted to describe OpenAtom as a state-funded vehicle, the 2025 accounts do not support it — 3.35 per cent of income is government subsidy and 88 per cent is corporate donation. Second, the Huawei concentration that was 37 per cent in 2023 is no longer the dominant fact of the donor base in 2025 by named entities, though the unresolved 18.95 million line makes that conclusion conditional. Third, and this is the observation the numbers almost conceal: “100 per cent from domestic legal persons, zero from foreign sources” is a legal classification, not a geographical one. Intel Asia-Pacific R&D is a top-ten donor in both 2024 and 2025 and is counted as domestic because it is a Chinese-registered subsidiary. The filing’s “zero foreign” is true and tells an outside reader less than it appears to2.

Institutional Analysis & Commentary:

This is the first time in six weeks of writing about this foundation that the numbers arrive without a caveat attached, and the caveat that has now been retired is the important part. The series has asked, in four separate briefings, for a per-project contribution table. It has not received one. What it has received instead is a complete, audited, six-year financial series — and the honest response to that is to notice that the series was asking for the wrong document, or rather for a document the foundation’s own accounting system does not generate.

First, credit the disclosure regime properly, because on the standard this series has been applying to everything else, it passes. Six consecutive years of annual audit reports, each paired with a special-information audit report, published on a public URL, signed by a named accounting firm, arithmetically reconcilable — income minus expenditure, cash-flow-to-balance-sheet, opening net assets matching the prior year’s close. The observatory that did the work verified these cross-checks year by year and the 2025 statements check out again: opening net assets CNY 162,185,865.27 in the 2025 report equals the end-2024 net assets stated in the 2024 report. An institution whose books balance across a year boundary in public is a different institution from one whose books balance only in the ministry’s file cabinet. KAIYUANSHE publishes a detailed but unaudited account through FY2021 and then stops; COPU publishes nothing; CAICT publishes policy but not money. On the measure of financial transparency, OpenAtom is not the worst actor in this ecosystem. It is the only actor.

Second, and here the analysis has to be precise about a distinction that the whole briefing turns on: this is audited money, not audited output. Read every line of the 2025 statement and count how many of them describe something a commons produces. Charitable activity cost CNY 129.16 million — a disbursement. Administration CNY 7.55 million — an overhead. Fixed and intangible assets CNY 18.30 million purchased — a capability. Not one line of the audited statements, and not one schedule of the special-information report, states how many patches merged, how many contributors were paid, how many releases shipped, or how many of the 64 hosted projects had a commit in the year. This is not a concealment. It is what a Chinese non-profit foundation’s statutory accounting is for: the annual-inspection regime under the charity law asks whether the money went where the charter said, and the answer here is yes and it is documented to the yuan. The instrument measures fiduciary compliance, and fiduciary compliance is a real thing that a donors’ trust must demonstrate. It is simply not the thing that tells you whether the commons is alive. The foundation is audited as a custodian. It is not audited as a maintainer, and no document in its disclosure set asks it to be.

Third, the 108-donor number deserves more than a passing mention, because it is the one line that argues against this series’ own previous conclusion. On 2026-11-26 this briefing read the published donation ladder and argued that the revenue structure — priced tiers, procurement-market donors — selects for projects a state-owned buyer can purchase, and named the pattern: every named commercial outcome in the foundation’s own June release is an OpenHarmony industry distribution. A donor base that grows from 47 to 108 organisations while Huawei’s named share falls from 37 per cent to roughly 6 is a real diversification, and diversification of the payer is the single most important precondition for a foundation to be more than an annex of one company. The honest reading, then, is not that the criticism was wrong in 2023 but that the 2025 filings show the foundation moving in the direction that would make it wrong. Two county-level construction-investment groups and a central power-grid SOE joining the donor list is not diversification away from the state — it is diversification within the state-adjacent sector — but Alibaba Cloud, Xiaomi, Hygon, iSoftStone and Intel APAC are a genuinely wider set of payers than openEuler’s and OpenHarmony’s vendors alone. A foundation whose donors are 108 companies is a consortium. A foundation whose donors are 7 companies and one of them gives a third of the money is a subsidiary. The 2025 filing moves OpenAtom from the second description toward the first, and the movement is documented, which is the only reason it can be credited.

Fourth, the capital expenditure line is the quiet anomaly and it should be flagged. CNY 18.30 million of fixed and intangible assets purchased in 2025 against CNY 1.31 million in 2024 — a fourteenfold increase, while charitable activity cost rose 37 per cent. Intangible assets on the balance sheet moved from CNY 8.81 million to CNY 18.86 million and fixed assets from CNY 9.37 million to CNY 14.20 million. The foundation is acquiring infrastructure — servers, licences, platform software, or something in that class — rather than routing the money out as programme spending. That is a legitimate move and it is also a strategic one: an institution that owns CI infrastructure is an institution that a project depends on, and dependency is a form of governance that no charter has to grant. Compared with the alternative — paying a vendor cloud bill every year — owning the infrastructure is the better institutional choice for a commons, because it converts a recurring payment into an asset the foundation controls. The question worth asking is only whether the same CNY 18.30 million of capability is available to a project that is not a founder’s flagship. That question is answerable from a per-project asset utilisation report, and no such report is published.

Fifth, the observation that connects this to everything else this month, and it is the uncomfortable one. The ecosystem’s two institutional paths — the ministry foundation and the municipal academy — have now been measured by two different instruments. Hangzhou built a nine-level talent standard so that a contribution could become a level a policy can read. OpenAtom built a six-year audited financial series so that a donation could become a tier a donor can be thanked at. Both instruments make something legible. Neither makes a contributor legible as a decision-maker. A foundation that can tell an outside reader, to the yuan, what each of 108 companies gave in 2025, and cannot tell that same reader which of its 64 projects was actively maintained, is not hiding anything — it is disclosing along the axis its regulator cares about. The regulator asks about fiduciary duty; the commons asks about maintenance; and where the two diverge, the filing follows the regulator. That is what a statutory disclosure regime does, everywhere, and it is why open-source foundations elsewhere publish both the audited accounts and a governance report that no regulator demanded.

Checkable questions.

(i) Will the foundation publish a per-project contribution table alongside the audited accounts — commits, contributors, share employed by the donating company, and any maintainer from outside it? The financial series has now set the standard the contribution table would have to meet: annual, comparable across years, published on a stable URL, and arithmetically checkable. There is no technical obstacle.

(ii) What is the CNY 18.95 million first line in the 2025 large-donor schedule, and is it one entity or an aggregate? An illegible name at 17 per cent of listed donations is a single OCR re-read away from either confirming or dissolving the “Huawei has diversified away” reading. The foundation could answer this in one sentence.

(iii) Does any Chinese non-profit foundation in this ecosystem publish a governance report that no regulator required — board composition, TOC membership, term lengths, and the method of appointment? The disclosure ladder now runs from work reports to audit reports to special-information audits. The missing rung is the one that says who decides.

🔬 Method — Who Reads the Filings: A Second Auditor Enters the Record#

1. The event, which is not an announcement by any Chinese institution. The Europe–China Digital Commons Observatory (中欧数字公地观察站), a research project publishing its full source files and data on GitHub under CC BY 4.0, maintains a research note titled Chinese open-source foundations and OpenAtom finances, first published 2026-11-03 and updated continuously — most recently 2026-11-26, ten hours before this briefing was written2. Its method, stated in the note itself, is unusual in a way worth naming: it checks pages the foundation published, finds that the PDFs are image-only scans, and runs OCR on them — pdftoppm at 200 to 300 dpi with tesseract chi_sim, with the raw OCR text committed to the repository. It assigns each fact an evidence level (A for an official filing, B for a reliable relay, C for self-reported, D for a lead) and marks every figure that has not yet been re-read against the page image.

2. What it found that a casual reader would not. Three of its corrections are worth recording because they show a method becoming self-correcting in public2:

  • A correction about the wrong page. The 3 November version concluded that no 2024 or 2025 report was published. On 9 November the observatory found them — on /information/audit rather than /information/work — and published the correction, including a note that the lead came from a separate review file. The 2025 figures in today’s first entry come from that correction.
  • A correction about an overstatement. A 3 November finding said OpenAtom’s 2026 coverage “does not mention international cooperation”. On 26 November it appended a qualification: KAIYUANSHE’s 2024 report records that OpenAtom hosted the Second Open Source Congress in Beijing on 25–26 August 2024 with the Linux Foundation, Eclipse Foundation, OSI, OpenInfra, OIN and the Rust Foundation. The finding was narrowed to the 2026 coverage and explicitly reframed as “a change from 2024 to 2026, not a constant absence”2.
  • A correction that weakens its own argument. The KAIYUANSHE report puts the 2023 OpenAtom competition’s prize total at CNY 13.384 million; OpenAtom’s audited competition spending that year was CNY 5.62 million. Rather than reconciling by assumption, the observatory recorded the gap as unreconciled and instructed readers: “Do not use 13.4 m as OpenAtom spending.”2

3. The claim it made about transparency, and it cuts in both directions. The observatory’s funding chapter closes with a finding that is the most useful sentence published about this subject all year3:

“No Chinese public open-source amount could be summed: China publishes per-project caps and rates, not budgets or disbursements (largest caps: Wuhan East Lake ¥30M, Shanghai ¥20M, Shenzhen ¥10M). No central-government open-source budget line was found.”

And on the European side, with equal candour: "[F] Documented EU public open-source money that can be summed: €11.5M (2023), €36M (2024), €19M (2025) — Germany and France only." Its conclusion: “Any headline ‘China spends X vs Europe Y’ is today unfounded in both directions.”

Institutional Analysis & Commentary:

An outside reader has now entered the record of Chinese open-source institutional analysis, and the way they entered is the finding — more than any number they produced.

First, name what actually happened, because it is a specific and rather elegant institutional event. A foundation published documents in a form its own public could not read: image-only PDFs, on a disclosure page, in a legal format nobody outside the charity-law regime is trained to parse. That is publication without legibility — technically compliant, practically opaque, and almost certainly not deliberate, since scanned filings are how the annual-inspection system has always worked. An external researcher then supplied the missing layer: OCR, structured tables, evidence grading, and committed source files. The disclosure existed. The readability did not. And readability, in the Ostrom vocabulary this series keeps reaching for, is the precondition of any boundary-crossing accountability: you cannot hold a body to account for a document you cannot read. The observatory did not acquire new information. It manufactured legibility out of information that was already public. That is a public good, produced by a foreign party, at its own expense, for an ecosystem that did not produce it for itself.

Second, and this is the part that matters institutionally: the counterpart to Chinese open-source institutions is now partly foreign, and it is not a ministry, a foundation, or a competitor. Note precisely what this observatory is. It is not an advocacy group; its funding chapter proposes EU–China cooperation on maintaining common upstreams and recommends “screen, don’t exclude” — engaging OpenAtom through neutral foundations with economic-security review, not disengagement. It is not uncritical; it concludes that OpenAtom “is financially a Huawei-anchored vehicle” and that “part of the donated money flows back to the donor as cloud-service revenue.” It is not hostile; it corrects its own overstatements in the open, and its standard for the Chinese side is the same standard it applies to Eclipse’s unreconciled member fees. It is an auditor without enforcement power, which is the only kind of auditor a foundation cannot refuse. This series has spent November documenting that Chinese open-source institutions are accountable upward to a ministry and outward to nobody. They have now acquired an outward account, and it was built by someone reading their own filings more carefully than anyone inside the ecosystem did.

Third, the honest reckoning for this series, which has published twenty-six briefings in November and used this observatory’s work as a source four times. The facts were public. The filings were on a public URL. The OCR was two commands and free. A briefing series that asked the same institution for a contribution table four times without ever OCR’ing its audit reports was not denied information — it was reading the announcements and not the accounts. That is a failure of method, not of access, and it is exactly the failure this series has been diagnosing in others: governing the artefact’s passage rather than its production, reading what is published for you rather than what is published at all. The observatory’s evidence-level discipline — A/B/C/D, and an explicit rule that self-reported figures are C unless they come from a filing — is a better epistemic standard than this briefing has been applying, and it cost one researcher with a GitHub repository and a tesseract binary.

Fourth, connect this to yesterday’s conclusion, because it revises it. On 2026-11-26 this briefing ended by asking whether KAIYUANSHE would publish its own governance “at the level now routinely published by the foundation it does not belong to”, and identified the publication of a per-project contribution table as the single missing document. That framing was too generous to the foundation and too hard on the community. The foundation does not routinely publish governance. It routinely publishes accounts, because a regulator requires it. The community publishes a detailed — if unaudited — annual report and then stopped after FY2021. Neither publishes the thing that matters, and the reason is the same for both: no regulator asks for it, and no donor pays for it. The difference between them is not ethical. It is that one of them has a statutory filing obligation and the other does not. An ecosystem in which legibility is a regulatory side-effect will be legible exactly where the regulator looks.

Fifth, the practical consequence for a foreign reader, stated plainly because the practical question is always the same. For anyone assessing whether to depend on an OpenAtom-hosted project, today’s documents add a fact and remove an illusion. The fact: the foundation is solvent, its overhead is 5.5 per cent, its donor base tripled in breadth in a year, its government dependence fell sharply, and its books reconcile. Those are the four things a custodian can be asked to demonstrate and it demonstrates all four. The illusion: that the same documents say anything about whether the projects work. They do not, and the fastest way to believe they do is to be impressed by the audit — a well-audited custodian of a dead commons and a well-audited custodian of a live one produce identical statements. The measurement that separates them is not in any filing reviewed in this run: a merged patch from someone who was not employed by the company that donated the project. One line in a public forge. That remains the only number this series has never been able to obtain from a document, and can obtain in a minute from a repository.

Checkable questions.

(i) Will the foundation’s own website carry text-searchable filings, rather than image scans, for FY2026? This is a capability question, not a policy one: the foundation runs CI infrastructure and code-compliance scanning as a hosted service. It has the tooling.

(ii) Will any Chinese institution — foundation, community or academy — publish an evidence-graded self-assessment of its own finances and governance, with corrections logged? The observatory’s methodology is public, its source repository is public, and the licence permits reuse. The barrier is not cost.

(iii) Does OpenAtom still host the Open Source Congress relationship it had in 2024, and where is that reported for 2026? The observatory’s correction to its own finding implies a real change between 2024 and 2026 that no Chinese source reviewed in this run has described either way.

🔍 Commentary — Who Reads the Filings, and What Reading Them Proves#

First: the week’s real finding is not a number, it is a division of labour that has quietly emerged. China’s open-source institutions produce documents: filings, work reports, standards, recognition lists, priced donation ladders, nine-level talent standards. They do not produce readings of those documents. The readings — OCR’d tables, cross-year reconciliations, evidence grades, corrections — are now being produced outside the ecosystem, in a public GitHub repository, under an open licence, by a researcher whose funding chapter recommends cooperation rather than decoupling. An institution that publishes without reading itself has outsourced its accountability to whoever is willing to do the reading, and that is not a criticism of the institution’s intentions. It is a description of where the accountability actually terminates.

Second, this closes a loop this series opened in October and could not close with Chinese sources. The recurring finding for two months has been that the ecosystem builds instruments that govern artefacts — licences, standards, certifications, export filings, assessments, price tiers — and never builds the instrument that governs a commons, which is a membership with a vote. Every briefing has ended by asking for the document that would prove otherwise. The document does not exist, and today’s audit series proves it structurally rather than rhetorically: six years of statements, complete to the yuan, containing no line item that could describe a vote. You cannot audit a franchise you do not have. The absence is not hidden in the filings. It is the shape of the filings.

Third, the genuine institutional innovation here is the second auditor, and its weakness is identical to its strength. The observatory checks filings, not behaviour. It can prove that CNY 128.84 million arrived from 108 donors; it cannot prove that any of the 64 hosted projects received a patch from a stranger. It measures the money side of the institution with high competence and the production side not at all — and the same sentence could be written about the foundation’s own auditors, its regulator, and this briefing series until this morning. The instrument that would close the gap is embarrassingly cheap: a script that queries each project’s default branch for commits and authors, runs monthly, and publishes the same table for every project. Nobody in this ecosystem has published it, and the reason is not technical difficulty. It is that the party with the data is also the party whose success metric would be disturbed by it.

Fourth, what to watch, and every item is checkable on one URL. Whether the FY2026 filings appear as text rather than scans — the difference between publishing and being readable. Whether the illegible CNY 18.95 million 2025 donor line is identified — one sentence that settles whether Huawei’s share continued to fall. Whether the CNY 18.30 million of capital expenditure is itemised by project — the first place a commons would give a non-flagship project access to infrastructure. Whether OpenAtom’s 2024 Open Source Congress relationship with the Linux Foundation, Eclipse, OSI and OIN has a 2026 counterpart — the observatory’s own correction implies a change nobody has reported in either direction. And, unchanged and unfulfilled since October: one merged patch, from one maintainer, employed by someone other than the project’s donor. That is the only number in this entire series that no filing can produce and every repository can.
