China Open Source Daily — 2026-11-13

On 2026-11-13 two instruments converted a person into something portable. OpenAtom opened registration for its open-source talent certification exam -- five tracks at the 2026 Developer Conference in Zhengzhou on 20-21 November, open-source general knowledge plus OpenHarmony engineer and senior engineer, openEuler engineer and openKylin engineer -- and stated plainly that the exam charges a fee, that all revenue is ring-fenced for building the certification system and open-source education, and that the credential makes capability 'measurable, recognisable and circulable.' The foundation's certification page shows four project ladders already standing behind the notice, each with three tiers ending at expert engineer, none of them a maintainer or governance track. The same day Global Times ran a feature pairing a genuine technical contribution with a national frame: ForgeKernel, the Tsinghua and ModelBest self-evolving kernel-generation system, is Apache-2.0 on GitHub (290 stars, 39 forks, 6 open issues, a published v1.5 TODO) with a CC BY 4.0 paper, and as of 2026-10-30 it led all three CANN-Bench 2.0 leaderboards on Ascend and NVIDIA's SOL-ExecBench FlashInfer-Bench track -- the first system to top both a Chinese and an American kernel leaderboard. Wrapped around it was the other instrument: 47 teachers from 34 countries who spent two weeks in Beijing on BAAI's FlagOS stack, part of a programme reporting 350+ applications from 48+ countries and pilot delivery with the African Union's ASRIC, framed by a Renmin University white paper arguing models, compute and corpora should be global digital public goods. One story sells a credential to a domestic developer; the other gives a foreign academic a capability. Both convert a person into a carrier. Neither converts a person into a maintainer -- and that is the week.

China Open Source Daily — 2026-11-13#

🏛️ Institutional Change — Tuition for the Commons: What the ¥ Certification Fee Buys, and What It Does Not#

1. The announcement, and why it is not a small story. On 2026-11-12 the OpenAtom Open Source Foundation (开放原子开源基金会) published a notice titled “Testing what you have learned, acting to support open source” announcing that on the sidelines of the 2026 OpenAtom Developer Conference (2026 开放原子开发者大会) — 20–21 November, Zhengzhou International Culture Exchange Centre — it will run a dedicated sitting of the OpenAtom Open Source Talent Certification examination (开放原子开源人才认证考试). Five certification tracks are offered: Open Source General Knowledge (开源通识), OpenHarmony Engineer, OpenHarmony Senior Engineer, openEuler Engineer, and openKylin Engineer. Eligibility is stated as developers, university teachers and students, enterprise technical staff, and employees of foundation ecosystem partners.

2. The sentence that carries the institutional content. The notice says the certification system exists so that “open-source capability can be measured, recognised and circulated (开源能力可度量、可认可、可流通)”, that it gives developers “a personal capability credibility credential” and gives industry “a unified yardstick.” And then, unhedged: “The certification exam charges a fee. All related revenue will be used entirely for building the certification system and the open-source education system, continuously supporting content updates, exam quality improvement and educational resources.” The foundation describes itself, in the same paragraph, as “a neutral, public-interest charitable organisation” whose operating philosophy is “co-build, co-share, co-govern, co-win” (共建·共享·共治·共赢), and frames sitting the exam as “an investment in your own growth and a contribution to the public-interest cause of open-source education.”

3. What was already standing behind the notice. OpenAtom’s certification page discloses a longer architecture than the notice does. Four project-specific certification schemes already exist — OpenHarmony (Engineer / Senior Engineer / Expert), openEuler, openKylin, and OpenTenBase — each organised as a three-tier ladder from engineer to expert, each described as supplying “a unified talent capability assessment standard” that links “talent, enterprises and the market.” On the OpenHarmony scheme the page states that “the exam syllabus and question bank were developed with contributions from over 20 experts from more than 14 organisations.” The notice’s five examination tracks are therefore a sitting of an existing ladder, not a new instrument: the general-knowledge track is new, the four project tracks are the certification page’s existing entries under another name.

4. The pattern this sits inside, which the source article also states. The same day’s material places the exam within a wider argument that China is opening its technology door rather than building a second wall — a framing covered separately below. Within that framing, the certification is one of three education instruments running in parallel this autumn: the certification fee (a credential priced per candidate), the FlagOS Global AI Open Compute Faculty Development Initiative (350+ teachers applied cumulatively across 48+ countries, two-week residencies in Beijing, delivered with the African Union’s ASRIC and Peking University — training the teachers who will carry the stack home), and BAAI’s push to co-develop AI-systems and compute-software-stack curricula with multiple universities, bringing in compute from Huawei, Tianshu Zhixin, Muxi and Jindie Shikong.

Institutional Analysis & Commentary:

A fee is not a scandal. A fee is an information structure, and the interesting question is what it prices.

Take the charitable defence at face value first, because it is substantially correct. A certification scheme costs money to operate and produces a public good. Building a syllabus, convening twenty-odd experts across fourteen organisations, writing and item-analysing a question bank, running invigilated sittings, maintaining a three-tier ladder across four operating systems — none of this is free, and none of it can be sold. Charging candidates and ring-fencing the revenue for the scheme is the standard, and arguably the honest, way to fund it. Compare the alternatives and the fee looks better than most: a state-funded certification creates an obligation to a ministry; a vendor-funded one creates an obligation to the vendor whose product the exam covers; a free one is either subsidised invisibly or quietly degraded. OpenAtom’s choice — charge the candidate, publish the ring-fence, call it public-interest — is a coherent third way, and the notice’s candour about the fee is itself a small institutional good. Most schemes bury it.

But now look at the location of the transaction, because that is where the institutional content is, and it is the same location this series has been measuring for a fortnight. Every instrument documented in the previous four briefings — the export registration queue, the ¥450 million foundation pledge, the ¥20.7 million restricted cultivation grant, the Xi’an adaptation certificate and its 245 million yuan procurement — pays for something at the moment an artefact or an actor crosses a boundary. Today’s exam is one more: a candidate pays to have a competence converted into a document. What none of these instruments price is the thing the open-source commons actually runs on: the review, the merge, the answer to a stranger’s question, the Thursday afternoon spent rejecting a bad patch kindly.

And the notice tells you this itself, in the choice of what is certified. Read the five tracks again: general knowledge, OpenHarmony engineer, OpenHarmony senior engineer, openEuler engineer, openKylin engineer. Every one of the four project tracks certifies competence in using or building on a Chinese domestic platform — not in maintaining, reviewing, or governing one. There is no track for “maintainer,” none for “release manager,” none for “governance committee member,” none for “licence compliance reviewer” — despite the foundation’s own OpenChain/SCA-adjacent ecosystem producing exactly such roles. The ladder’s top rung is expert engineer, and an expert engineer is a person who knows the system well enough to build with it. The certification therefore codifies, precisely, the outbound competence — and leaves the inbound competence uncertified, unpriced, and unfunded. That is not an oversight unique to OpenAtom; it is the same asymmetry, now with a price list attached.

Third, the deeper economics, and it is not about the money. A credential is a signal, and signalling systems have an unavoidable property: they are worth something only if they are costly, and they are harmful only if the cost is uncorrelated with the thing being signalled. Spence’s original point was that education can function as a signal whether or not it raises productivity, provided it is cheaper for the more able to obtain. A fee-based credential imports an additional filter that Spence did not put there: willingness to pay. For a developer in Shenzhen earning a competitive salary, a few hundred yuan to put a foundation’s credential on a CV is a rational purchase. For the university student in a provincial city who is the actual marginal contributor to an open-source project — the person whose first patch matters most and whose budget is thinnest — a fee is a filter on the wrong variable. The foundation’s answer, that the fee funds education for everyone, is real but indirect: it redistributes from candidates to the scheme, not from funded candidates to unfunded ones. A voucher scheme — the exact instrument Xi’an used for SME adaptation on 2026-11-11 — would have solved the same problem and was not chosen, because a voucher requires someone to decide who is worthy, and a price requires only that someone can pay.

Fourth, the honest counterweight, and there is a real one. Somebody has to pay the reviewers, and the ecosystem has finally noticed. The certification revenue is ring-fenced for content updates, exam quality and educational resources — which, read generously, is the first budget line in this entire run of briefings that funds the people who know the code well enough to set the standard. Twenty experts from fourteen organisations writing an exam syllabus is a real, if narrow, form of upstream work: they are reading the platform closely enough to test others on it. And the general-knowledge track is genuinely new in kind — a foundation asserting that there is a body of knowledge called “open source” that is not identical to any one product, and that can be assessed independently of vendor. That is a claim about open source as a discipline, and it is a more interesting claim than any of the project ladders make.

Fifth, the prediction, and it is falsifiable. The test of whether this is a credential market or an institution is not the pass rate. It is what the credential is accepted for. If, within twelve months, a foundation credential appears as a hard requirement in a public procurement specification or as a substitute for documented contribution history in a maintainer appointment, the ecosystem has built a ladder to jobs and the ladder will determine who gets them. If it remains a line on a CV that employers treat as a nice-to-have, it is a service the foundation sells and a signal candidates buy, and the commons is unchanged. The telling detail will be whether any project’s CONTRIBUTING or governance document ever cites the certification — because that is the moment a purchased signal becomes an institutional gate. No Chinese project has done that yet. The voucher-design alternative — and the missing maintainer track — tell you which way the designers were thinking.

Checkable questions.

(i) What does a sitting cost, per track, and is there any fee waiver, scholarship or institutional bulk-purchase route? The notice does not publish the price. The price is the filter; publishing it is the minimum condition for anyone outside the ecosystem to judge who the filter excludes.

(ii) Will the pass lists, item statistics, or failure rates be published? A credential whose failures are invisible is a credential that cannot be evaluated. If nobody ever fails, the fee is buying a certificate, not an assessment.

(iii) Does any track’s syllabus contain a unit on maintaining an upstream — reviewing a contribution, triaging an issue, deciding a licence question, participating in a governance vote? On the published descriptions, none does. That absence is the finding, and it is the same absence this series has now recorded in six consecutive instruments.

🏗️ Institutional Change — The Export as a Public Good: ForgeKernel’s Dual-Leaderboard Kernel Agent, and the Faculty Programme That Trains Its Foreign Users#

1. The event, sourced from outside the WeChat ecosystem. On 2026-11-12 Global Times published a long feature under the headline “The US tightens AI chip restrictions on China; why is China instead opening the technology door?” The factual core is verifiable independently: a Tsinghua University and ModelBest (面壁智能) research team released ForgeKernel, a self-evolving GPU-kernel generation system, whose paper arXiv:2611.04998 was submitted 2026-11-03 and is licensed CC BY 4.0. The code is published at github.com/OpenBMB/ForgeKernel under Apache-2.0, created 2026-10-30, with 290 stars and 39 forks as of this writing. The abstract claims, on NVIDIA Hopper and Blackwell, parity or better against its expert reference on 25 of 27 operators across 29 configurations at a +10% geometric mean; on Ascend 910C, a +25.1% geometric-mean lead over the per-operator public best across all eight level-4 operators of the CANN-Bench Season 1 final leaderboard, with its full-suite submission ranked first on the A3 leaderboard; and — as of 2026-10-30 — first place on all three CANN-Bench 2.0 solution leaderboards (Ascend 910C, 950DT, 950PR) and on NVIDIA’s SOL-ExecBench FlashInfer-Bench track. The repository’s news section states outright: “We reached #1 on the FlashInfer-Bench leaderboard of NVIDIA SOL-ExecBench” and “We reached #1 on Huawei CANN-Bench (Ascend A3).” The dual-leaderboard claim is true, and it is the first one in this domain.

2. What the system does, in the terms its authors use, because the mechanism is the story. Kernels are the operators through which a model’s arithmetic reaches silicon. The feature quotes Li Yuxuan (李宇轩), ModelBest’s chief scientist for intelligent evolution: “Building the hardware is only the first step; whether a chip can fully release its performance depends on whether the accompanying software has been continuously and thoroughly polished.” And the ratio that carries the piece: silicon is the engine, the software stack is the gearbox and the road. Traditionally, writing a top-tier kernel required an engineer who understood the algorithm, the chip architecture, memory and instruction scheduling, with adaptation cycles measured in months or years. ForgeKernel’s technical novelty is stated in the abstract with unusual precision: it does not imitate source code, it profiles a reference kernel’s hardware behaviour and distils the validated architectural decisions into reusable mechanism knowledge, then seals each accepted kernel as the next run’s reference, so that architecture can be revisited rather than fixed early. The README quantifies the consequence: every promoted reference was beaten again, by up to +9.9% per generation. Li’s formulation: “Previously it was people making AI stronger; from now on it is AI making itself stronger.”

3. The other half of the article, which the headline does not mention but which is the institutional payload. In October 2026, 47 teachers from 46 universities and institutions across 34 countries came to Beijing for a two-week Global AI Open Compute Faculty Development Initiative (全球AI开放计算师资发展计划). After finishing, the feature reports, some planned to open courses at home, some to build AI compute laboratories, some to keep contributing to Chinese open-source communities. The programme sits on BAAI’s FlagOS stack; the FlagOS site reports 350+ cumulative teacher applications from 48+ countries and regions, and states that the model was piloted with the African Union’s ASRIC and Peking University before global recruitment. In the same month the China AI International Application and Governance Cooperation White Paper (《中国人工智能国际应用与治理合作白皮书》), compiled by bodies including Renmin University’s Gaoling School of AI, proposed treating models, compute and corpora as global digital public goods, moving cooperation “from technology export to capability co-building,” to help developing countries with AI that is “unusable, unaffordable, uncontrollable.” The three delivery paths FlagOS’s Zhu Qigang (朱其罡) describes are concrete: expand online compute supply; connect institutions with Chinese chips and equipment; and for regions with limited money, power and operations capacity, explore device-side lightweight solutions. His stated test is the best sentence in the article: “A system that can only run in a few large laboratories can hardly be called inclusive. Only when it can be used stably across multiple chips, limited resources and different environments does an ecosystem really have vitality.”

4. The industrial-policy spine, stated as such. The feature anchors the work in the 15th Five-Year Plan outline, which calls for strengthening “efficient supply of compute, algorithms and data” and "‘model–chip–cloud–application’ (模芯云用) coordinated innovation." And it quotes Sun Chenghao (孙成昊) of Tsinghua’s Center for Strategic and Security Studies: China is “shifting from responding to external compute-supply pressure to building a compute system with multiple technical routes and supply choices”; self-reliance “enables China to provide sustainable, maintainable technology for international cooperation, while open source and joint R&D let partners participate in the improvement process.” Zhu closes: “We must keep the initiative for development in our own hands, and also let more people participate.”

Institutional Analysis & Commentary:

Here is a contribution that passes every ordinary test this series applies — and the instrument wrapped around it is the one that should make an open-source reader uneasy. Both things are true, and the entry is about holding them at once.

Start by crediting the artefact, without reservation. ForgeKernel is Apache-2.0 code in a public repository that anyone can clone today, plus a CC BY 4.0 paper, and it leads both the Ascend and the NVIDIA kernel-generation leaderboards — a genuinely hard claim, because it means the same architecture-first search works against two entirely different ISAs and memory hierarchies. That is the most portable piece of AI infrastructure to emerge from China in this series. It also makes the kind of contribution that is costly and non-sunk: kernels are not scraped from a product line and dumped; they are produced by an agent loop whose whole point is to beat the previous best. This is not a platform donation of already-paid-for code. And the repository is thin-but-honest — 290 stars, 39 forks, one principal contributor, six open issues, a v1.5 TODO list published in the open with checkboxes. Compare that with the 12,000-star project with no replies this series examined on 2026-11-09: this project tells you exactly what it has not done yet, in public. That is a small but real institutional virtue, and it is the opposite of the metric-driven posture.

Now the instrument, and it is the one thing here that is not a contribution. The ForgeKernel announcement and the faculty programme appear in the same article, under a national-competition frame — tighter US chip controls, a Reuters-reported push by US legislators on 10 November, an FT-reported NVIDIA requirement that some cloud partners reserve at least 51% of contracted services for non-Chinese firms — and are then read as evidence that China “did not build another wall,” choosing instead to “let technology capability travel to the world through open-source cooperation.” The pairing is the instrument. Open-sourcing a kernel agent is a technical act with a technological rationale (adaptation cycles from months to days, portability across vendors). Placing it in a bilateral-restriction narrative converts it into a foreign-policy demonstration: the release becomes evidence in an argument about which country is open. The code is genuinely open; the narrative does the work; and the two are not the same kind of claim. This is the mechanism this series named on 2026-11-12 in the export-control context — the state has not endorsed open source as a mode of production; it has identified open-weight publication as the lowest-friction channel for a national capability to travel — here stated from the other side: the contribution is real and it is being billed as diplomacy.

Second, and this is the part an institutional economist should dwell on, because it is genuinely elegant and genuinely worrying at the same time. The faculty programme does something none of the instruments in the previous fortnight can do: it manufactures the counterpart. A commons requires users; a stack requires a curriculum; a curriculum requires teachers; teachers require two weeks in Beijing. FlagOS’s own numbers make the design legible: 350+ applications from 48+ countries, 18+ adapted chip vendors, 18 million downloads — and a pilot with the African Union’s scientific council and Peking University that produced, per the ASRIC call, 130+ applications from 24 African countries in the first cohort. The white paper’s shift “from technology export to capability co-building” is, read coldly, the most sophisticated form of technology transfer in the entire ecosystem, because a trained teacher is a durable asset that no export licence can revoke and no re-export control can reach. The teachers go home and teach the stack; their students arrive at the stack already fluent. Where the model-export regime needs a registration queue to move a capability across a border, the faculty programme moves the capability into people who cross borders freely. Ostrom would say this is exactly right: institutions are reproduced by people who have learned them, and a commons with trained participants on four continents is more durable than one with a fast lane.

Third, the asymmetry again, and this time it is not a gap but a design. Zhu’s line — “feedback from different hardware, languages and application scenarios will in turn test the compatibility and usability of Chinese open-source technology” — is a genuine commons argument, and it is the best defence of the whole programme. But note who is invited to do what. Teachers are invited to use, teach and test; they are invited to bring their problems back; they are, per FlagOS’s curriculum, trained in software stack, compute frameworks, chip architecture, kernel optimisation, compilers, communication and model-system co-design — a consumer-and-adaptor curriculum, admirably deep on how to make the stack work on your hardware. What no published description of the programme offers is a role in the project’s governance: no maintainer track, no committer path, no seat on a technical steering committee, no stated route from “teacher who can run FlagOS” to “contributor whose patch lands in FlagOS.”** That is not hypocrisy; it is a delivery model, and the difference between a delivery model and a commons is precisely whether the recipient can change the thing. The programme is, in the strict sense, the best-designed distribution channel this ecosystem has built — and distribution is not governance. Zhu’s own formulation is honest about the limit: the point is that “more people participate.” Participation in use, yes. Participation in direction, unspecified.

Fourth, the honest scoreboard, because this entry would be dishonest if it ended on the caveat. Set today’s story against the fortnight that preceded it and the comparison is stark. The export fast lane prices nothing and answers no inbound patch; the ¥450 million foundation pledge funds its own projects’ rent; the ¥20.7 million grant buys testing and hosting; the Xi’an certificate ends in a procurement contract. ForgeKernel costs the state nothing, gives away capability at real marginal cost, and is accepted by the same foreign leaderboards it intends to beat. The faculty programme is the only instrument in this entire run whose output is human capability located outside China and beyond the reach of any registry. If the criterion for a commons is an asset made non-excludable by someone who could have kept it, ForgeKernel and the faculty programme both pass, and the foundation’s certification fee does not. That is worth saying plainly, because the reflex — treat every Chinese institutional announcement as an instrument of industrial policy — is a framework that has stopped discriminating. The discriminating question is not who arranged the press coverage; it is whether the asset is excludable after the fact and whether outsiders can change it. On the first, ForgeKernel is squarely a gift. On the second, the answer is not yet published.

Fifth, the prediction, in falsifiable form. If, within twelve months, a paper or a kernel contributed by a team outside China lands in ForgeKernel’s kernels/ directory or its knowledge base, the project has crossed from donated capability to working commons. If the repository’s contributor graph stays dominated by its founding lab while the download and star counts grow, it will have become the thing this series has documented six times: an authoritative reference that many consume and few change. The reading is available to anyone with git log — which is the whole advantage of judging artefacts over press releases. And there is a second, sharper test: when a foreign university that received a FlagOS-trained teacher proposes a change to FlagOS itself, whose name is on the merge? The faculty programme has already put 47 teachers on four continents; the answer to that question determines whether it trained users or colleagues.

Checkable questions.

(i) Has any contributor outside Tsinghua and ModelBest landed a kernel, a knowledge-base entry, or a merged change in ForgeKernel? The repository is public and the answer is a git log away. At 39 forks and 290 stars, the project is small enough that the answer will be unambiguous for some months yet.

(ii) Do the CANN-Bench and FlashInfer-Bench leaderboard entries remain first-party submissions only, or has an external team submitted using the released system? A benchmark led by one’s own agent is not the same as a benchmark others use one’s agent to win.

(iii) What do the 47 teachers teach when they get home, and with whose hardware? FlagOS’s own three-path plan concedes the binding constraint — some lack devices, some lack courses, some cannot obtain compute after returning. A programme that trains teachers who then cannot run the stack has transferred a curriculum, not a capability. The follow-up data to ask for is course adoption at the 46 home institutions in the 2027 academic year.

🔍 Commentary — Who Gets to Be the Counterpart#

First, today’s two stories are the same instrument at two price points, and the prices are the finding. A candidate pays a fee to have competence certified; a teacher is paid (in training, in compute, in curriculum) to carry a stack abroad. Both convert a person into a document or a carrier of capability, and neither converts a person into a maintainer. The foundation charges the domestic developer for a credential that ends at “expert engineer”; the faculty programme gives a foreign academic two weeks of the strongest compute-systems training available in Beijing and sends them home to teach it. The domestic transaction is a purchase; the international one is an investment. That asymmetry is not accidental and it is not sinister — it is the same asymmetry this series has now recorded in seven consecutive instruments: the ecosystem pays, without hesitation, for the outbound half of the commons and does not yet have a budget line for the inbound half. An outbound person is a carrier. An inbound person is a reviewer with standing.

Second, credit where the week earned it, because this week earned more than last. ForgeKernel is Apache-2.0, CC BY 4.0, on GitHub, leading both the Ascend and the NVIDIA leaderboards, with its unfinished work published as a TODO list. That is a better specimen of open source than anything else in this run of briefings — better than the ¥450 million pledge, better than the platinum-donor donation of an already-shipped OS, better than a fast lane that makes publication a filing. It is genuinely costly work given away at real marginal cost, by a lab that could have kept it, and it is accepted by the foreign benchmarks it intends to beat. A framework that cannot say this is a bad framework. The same is true of the faculty programme, and Zhu’s test sentence deserves repeating because it would be a good sentence in any ecosystem: a system that can only run in a few large laboratories can hardly be called inclusive.

Third, and then the question that makes the week legible. Who is the counterpart? Every institution in this run — the register, the certificate, the voucher, the donation, the faculty residency, the certification fee — is designed from the point of view of the party initiating a transaction: the ministry that registers, the city that certifies, the company that donates, the lab that teaches, the foundation that examines. An open-source commons is the only institution in the set that is designed from the point of view of the party on the other end — the stranger whose patch arrives at 3 a.m., whose competence was never certified, whose teacher never visited Beijing, who has no standing in any registry and whose only claim is that they read the code carefully and found a bug. Nothing published this fortnight says what happens to them. That is not a moral criticism of any of the six instruments, each of which does a real job. It is an observation about who the designs are for, and it is the whole of the difference between a capability-management system and a commons.

Fourth, the sentence to keep. In the export-control briefing on 2026-11-12 this series set a test that will take until 2027 to resolve: whether a patch arrives from someone with no relationship to the founding company and somebody whose job description includes that queue replies the same week. Today’s stories make that test more concrete and, in one respect, closer: ForgeKernel gives that stranger something to patch. A 290-star Apache-2.0 repository with six open issues and a published TODO list is the most patchable artefact in this entire run — it has explicitly told the world what it has not done. So the test is now within reach of any reader of this briefing with a GPU and an afternoon: clone it, pick a v1.5 checkbox, submit, and see who answers. The interesting outcome is not the merge. It is the reply.
