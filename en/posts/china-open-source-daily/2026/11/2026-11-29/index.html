⚠️ Editorial note: The open source ecosystem in China operates under a distinct institutional framework — characterized by state-led initiatives, intranet-like boundaries, and top-down governance. Readers should be aware that this context differs from the community-driven open source model common in other regions. The term “open source” as used in Chinese media may refer to practices that diverge from the conventional definition.

China Open Source Daily — 2026-11-29#

🏛️ Governance — The Country in the Name: OpenAtom Becomes “International,” and What That Word Would Have to Mean#

1. The announcement, and where it was made, because the venue is half the content. On the afternoon of 2026-11-28, speaking at the 2026 OpenHarmony Technical Conference (2026 开源鸿蒙技术大会) at the Yangtze International Convention Centre in Nanjing, Xie Shaofeng (谢少锋), chair of the board of OpenAtom Open Source Foundation (开放原子开源基金会), announced that with state approval the foundation had formally renamed itself the “OpenAtom International Open Source Foundation” (开放原子国际开源基金会), with “internationalisation” (国际化) now written into its core mission1. Xinhua Daily’s account of the same speech is blunter about who approved it: “the state has approved the foundation’s renaming” (国家已批准基金会更名)2. A national foundation acquired the word “international” in its registered name, announced from a provincial capital, at the annual conference of one of its projects, by a chairman whose career was in the ministry that supervises it.

2. The financial commitment in the same speech, juxtaposed because the two sentences are one sentence. In the same address Xie committed the foundation to spending nearly CNY 100 million on OpenHarmony ecosystem construction in 2026, and “no less than CNY 150 million a year” in the years following1. Set that against the audited 2025 income statement read in this series on 2026-11-27 — total donations CNY 149 million, government subsidy CNY 4.88 million, 108 corporate donors — and the arithmetic is not ambiguous: the annual OpenHarmony commitment alone is roughly the size of the foundation’s entire 2025 income3. Whichever way that gap is closed, it is closed by a party holding more than a donation.

3. The structural facts about the foundation that the rename leaves untouched, all published by the foundation itself. OpenAtom’s own “About Us” page, read on 2026-11-29, describes it as “a non-profit institution dedicated to advancing the global open-source cause, founded in Beijing in June 2020” and as “a developer-centred open-source project incubation platform and science-and-technology public-welfare service institution.” Its stated project portfolio is OpenHarmony (IoT), openEuler (digital infrastructure), openKylin (desktop), OpenTenBase (database), OpenLoong (humanoid robotics) and OpenLET (embodied-intelligence datasets). Its business scope is enumerated as “fundraising, special-purpose grants, publicity and promotion, education and training, academic exchange, compliance support, open-source ecosystem construction, open-source infrastructure, and international cooperation.” And its governance, in the foundation’s own words, is:

  • 理事会 (Board of Directors) — “responsible for deliberating and amending the foundation’s charter”; the chair, vice-chairs and secretary-general are elected from among the directors; the chair is the foundation’s legal representative.
  • 技术监督委员会 (Technical Oversight Committee, TOC) — a “neutral technical decision-making body” responsible for technical decisions and project incubation review.
  • 开源安全委员会 (Open Source Security Committee) — authorised by the board to plan and coordinate security process, projects, tools, standards and external cooperation.
  • 秘书处 (Secretariat) — the executive body, with nine departments: business development, operations, legal and IP, brand promotion, R&D, finance, general affairs, education and training, and industry research.

The published roster is the point. The list of directors and supervisors on that page comprises: Xie Shaofeng (chair; formerly director-general of MIIT’s Information Technology Development Department and MIIT’s Science and Technology Department, and MIIT chief engineer), Zhang Shunmao (vice-chair; senior VP, Huawei), Wang Juhong (vice-chair; chair of Tencent’s technical committee), Liu Xiangwen (vice-chair; VP, Alibaba Cloud), Xiao Xue (vice-chair; executive president and chief engineer, Inspur), Cheng Xiaoming (director and secretary-general; formerly deputy director of MIIT’s General Office, party secretary of a ministry-affiliated research institute, and a former municipal vice-mayor), Peng Jiang (director; executive president, Chinasoft International), Han Naiping (director; chief scientist, KylinSoft), Wang Chenglu (director; CEO, Shenzhen Kaihong), Jiang Tao (director; founder and chairman, CSDN), Huang Dongxu (director; co-founder and CTO, PingCAP), Liu Wenhuan (director; general manager, UnionTech), and Gao Sumei (supervisor; secretary-general, China Information Technology Industry Federation). TOC chair: Tan Zhongyi (谭中意)4.

4. What is absent from the governance page, enumerated rather than asserted. There is no membership class, no voting members, no contributor electorate, no election procedure and no dates, no published charter text, no committee minutes, and no per-project governance document. Directors are elected by directors. The one body described as “neutral” is the TOC, and its stated function is technical guidance and incubation review, not the allocation of rights. The foundation publishes an org chart. It does not publish a constitution.

5. Two more facts from the same day, because they complete the picture rather than decorate it. Xie’s framing of the foundation’s own history, as reported: OpenHarmony is the foundation’s flagship project and the foundation was created by OpenHarmony — “China’s first national-level open-source foundation was thus born… a milestone in the history of Chinese open-source development.” In 2020 Huawei contributed the underlying technology of the OpenHarmony operating system “to the country” (贡献给国家), and the foundation undertook its incubation; in 2025 OpenHarmony formally graduated (毕业), which Xie reads as proof that “China’s open-source foundation now has the capacity to cultivate open-source projects with international influence.”1 And the second: at the same conference, Huawei’s terminal-BG chairman Yu Chengdong (余承东) described Huawei’s own change of status — from “technical contributor” to “full-domain user” (全域使用者) of OpenHarmony — disclosing more than 100 industry distributions, 2,500-plus hardware and software products that have passed compatibility testing, Huawei terminal devices built on OpenHarmony past 100 million units this month, and OpenHarmony-based intelligent hardware exceeding 1.4 billion units5. A foundation whose board includes the vendor whose code it hosts; a vendor that has moved from contributing the code to consuming it; a “neutral” technical body that certifies the products of that vendor’s ecosystem.

Institutional Analysis & Commentary:

The interesting question about a name is not whether it is honest. It is what the name entitles the holder to do, and whether anyone outside can check. On both counts, “international” is the one word in this rename that can be tested — so let us test it.

First, name the arrangement in Coase’s terms, because this is his 1974 problem with the sign reversed. Coase’s The Lighthouse in Economics was written to puncture a textbook claim — that lighthouses are the canonical public good the state must provide because no private party would. He went to the British records and found that Trinity House, a chartered body financed by light dues levied on shipping, had provided the service for centuries; the generalisation he drew was that the boundary between public provision and voluntary association is an empirical question about which arrangement lowers the cost of provision, not a theorem.6 Read the Nanjing announcement in that frame and it is not a scandal. It is a lighthouse with a new signboard. A body chartered by the state, funded by levies on the parties who use it — corporate donors at a published price ladder (no less than CNY 10 million, 3 million, 1 million and 200,000 a year at the four tiers, as this series read on 2026-11-26) — delivering a service those users need: a neutral legal home and a trademark for a shared codebase. Coase’s point was that this can work. His point was never that it is a commons. A lighthouse is a club good with a toll: everyone who passes benefits, and nobody who passes gets a vote on where the light points.

Second, the word that matters in the rename is not “international” and not “foundation.” It is “renamed.” A name change is a registration act — it alters what the entity may write on a contract, not what it may do. This deserves an entry rather than a line because of the gap between the rename and the four things an “international” open-source foundation actually needs, each of which is a document rather than a title:

  • (i) Legal personality abroad. An international foundation needs the capacity to hold assets, sign agreements, receive deductible donations and be sued in the jurisdictions where its contributors live. A Chinese-registered public-welfare foundation has none of that outside China. The test is concrete: does it incorporate a sister entity, open a foreign account, or obtain charitable status anywhere else?
  • (ii) A published charter with an amendment procedure. The foundation’s own description says the board is “responsible for amending the charter” — which tells you the charter exists, and that the body it constrains is the body that may amend it. An international participant cannot assess a governance commitment it cannot read.
  • (iii) A membership with votes. Not a donor tier — a membership class with defined admission, defined rights and a defined vote, including on board composition. Without one, “international” describes where the conference speakers come from, not who decides anything.
  • (iv) Work a foreign maintainer can do. The operational test this series has used all November: a merged patch from someone outside the founding company, and a CODEOWNERS file naming a non-founding-company maintainer. A rename does not merge a patch.

Third, Olson’s Logic of Collective Action, because it explains why the rename happened and why it is not merely cynical. Olson’s 1965 argument: in a large group the collective good will not be provided by voluntary contribution, because each member’s share of the benefit is smaller than the cost of contributing; provision requires either selective incentives or a privileged member for whom the good is worth supplying alone.7 Chinese open source has exactly one privileged member. The number in Xie’s speech that is really an Olson number is CNY 150 million a year, guaranteed, against a foundation whose total 2025 income was CNY 149 million: no coalition of 108 donors produced that; one privileged party underwrote it. That is not a failure of the ecosystem — it is the predictable equilibrium of a large group with one dominant beneficiary, and it explains why graduation, compatibility counts and distribution counts all look like success while the membership question remains unasked. A privileged member does not need members. It needs a legal vehicle, and it now has one with the word “international” on it.

Fourth, and this is what makes the rename more than a branding story: an org chart that omits a membership class is not an incomplete org chart — it is a complete statement of governance. Look again at what the page does contain. A board that amends the charter and elects itself. A TOC described as neutral that reviews incubation. A security committee authorised by the board. Nine secretariat departments, including legal and IP, brand promotion, and industry research. In Hansmann’s terms this is a nonprofit under a non-distribution constraint — no residual claimant, hence no owner to distribute to — and the constraint is real and verifiable from the audited statements8. But the non-distribution constraint answers only “who cannot take the surplus.” It is silent on “who directs the surplus,” and the answer is: a board, elected by itself, chaired by a former MIIT director-general, on which the largest domestic platform and hardware vendors and one state industry federation sit. That is governance by a constituency of funders and supervisors, and it is a coherent design. What it is not is a commons, and no rename alters a constituency. Ostrom’s design principles begin with clearly defined boundaries — who is in and who is out — and include collective-choice arrangements, meaning that most individuals affected by the rules can participate in modifying them9. A foundation whose rule-makers are its funders and its supervisors, with no class of affected users able to elect anyone, is not a common-pool institution however its mission statement reads. Nothing in the announcement suggests anyone intended otherwise.

Fifth, the honest counter-argument, given its full weight. There is a real sense in which the rename is an institutional improvement rather than a fig leaf: it changes what the foundation is authorised to do. A registered public-welfare foundation in China operates within a narrow business scope, and “international cooperation” sitting inside that scope is what allows currency to leave, agreements to be signed, foreign partners to be hosted and events convened abroad. Compare COSCon'26, covered here on 2026-11-26, where the international participation amounted to five foreign speakers on a stage supported by a district talent office. If the renamed foundation can now fund foreign maintainers, host foreign projects and hold contractual relationships across borders, that is a capability the ecosystem did not have last week — and it is precisely the capability the ecosystem’s own blue paper asked for when it announced its aim of moving “from participant toward ecosystem builder and rule co-builder in the global AI competition.” An outside reader should therefore record the possibility that this is the real thing, because there is a cheap way to find out. The annual audit report — published each year on openatom.org/information/audit, the 2025 pair uploaded 2026-08-19 — contains the foundation’s activity statement. If internationalisation has substance, it will appear as an expenditure line, a foreign entity, or a grant to a non-Chinese recipient. That is a check a stranger can run in an afternoon, and it is the first time in this series’ record that a Chinese foundation has made a claim testable by its own next filing.

Checkable questions.

(i) When is the renamed foundation’s registered name actually changed in the civil-affairs registry, and does the English name appear on any contract or filing? The foundation’s own website still reads 开放原子开源基金会 as of 2026-11-29.

(ii) Does the foundation publish its charter — the 章程 — in full, with the amendment procedure and the board election procedure? Everything else about governance is derivative of this document, and it is the one document the foundation describes as existing but does not publish.

(iii) Is there a membership class at all — and if the answer is “donors and project communities,” are the community representatives elected or appointed?

(iv) Does the 2026 audit report contain a line for international expenditure, a foreign subsidiary, or a grant outside mainland China?

(v) Does internationalisation change the OpenHarmony compatibility regime for non-Chinese products? That is what the word has to mean operationally for anyone outside.

📊 The Ledger — Four Sub-National Instruments on One Day: What a Federation Looks Like Without a Treaty#

1. Four instruments, and the last one is the only one with a price printed on it. On 2026-11-28, in Nanjing, alongside the foundation’s rename:

  • A municipal action plan with a revenue target. The Nanjing OpenHarmony Intelligent Agent Development Action Plan (2026–2028) (南京市开源鸿蒙智能体发展行动计划) was published, deploying nine key tasks — agent product development, ecosystem-enterprise cultivation, public-service system, industrial park, “characteristic OpenHarmony OPC community,” a secure-and-trustworthy technology support system, benchmark scenario development, and talent-system cultivation — with three targets for end-2028: more than ten benchmark scenarios, more than 100 ecosystem enterprises, and enterprise revenue above CNY 100 billion, to build “a nationally leading OpenHarmony agent innovation highland.”10 The city also published ten designated scenario domains — metro, urban construction, industry, culture and sport, healthcare, education, policing, water, power and smart parks — and stated its baseline: 2025 software business revenue of about CNY 1.02 trillion, up 16.7 per cent, with 15 certified OpenHarmony distributions and 122 certified products, both second nationally1011.
  • A municipal reception body. The Nanjing Open Source Ecosystem Promotion Centre (南京开源生态促进中心) was formally established, with eight figures unveiling the plaque. Its stated tasks: adaptation verification, scenario incubation, talent cultivation and ecosystem aggregation, under a mechanism named on stage as “enterprise-led, government-guided, university-empowered, alliance-enabled” (企业主导、政府指导、高校赋智、联盟赋能)211.
  • A certification-capacity expansion. The conference closed with an unveiling ceremony for OpenHarmony compatibility-assessment partner centres (兼容性测评合作中心) — “a new batch of authoritative assessment institutions” added “to strengthen the force of official assessment.”*11
  • A corporate programme that pays, in three currencies, for adoption. Hongtu Plan (鸿图计划) — “initiated by the Global Intelligent IoT Alliance (GIIC), fully supported by Huawei” — was described by GIIC deputy secretary-general He Xiaoyu (何晓宇) as aggregating chip, module, OSV and device partners through three incentive dimensions: development incentives, assessment incentives and commercialisation incentives (开发激励、测评激励、商用激励). Eight companies signed on stage: Aima Technology (爱玛科技), Shanghai Weipai Ge (威派格, smart water), Shanghai Yuankong Automation, Baoding Feiling Embedded, Beijing Shenzhou Shihan, Hangzhou Hexing Electrical, Hangzhou Meiyi Automation and the Beijing Agricultural Machinery Research Institute — spanning two-wheelers, water, industrial hardware and IoT terminals. It sits under a Huawei executive’s framing of the vendor’s own role as “from technical contributor to full-domain enabler (全域使能者)”, with the case study being China’s 450-million-unit domestic two-wheeler fleet, where OpenHarmony is pitched at “fragmented intelligence, inconsistent experience and non-interoperable ecosystems.”**12

2. And the numbers all four instruments rest on, given by the programme’s own leadership on the day. Chen Haibo (陈海波), chair of the OpenHarmony programme’s Technical Steering Committee and a Huawei Fellow, gave: contributors up 55–58 per cent year on year to more than 14,300 (14,550 as of mid-November); more than 2,600 hardware and software products through compatibility assessment, double the previous year; more than 120 industry distributions in production; device platform 1.0, embodied intelligence 2.0 (RoboOH / 具身智能 2.0) and unified interconnect 3.0 released together; and code volume from 7 million lines at inception to 150 million, of which 40 million were added last year1112. Yu Chengdong’s figures the same day: more than 100 industry distributions, 2,500-plus products through compatibility testing, Huawei terminals on OpenHarmony past 100 million units, OpenHarmony-based intelligent hardware past 1.4 billion units5. The two distribution counts — “more than 120” from the TSC chair and “more than 100” from the vendor — are not the same quantity, and neither was defined on stage. Yan Binyu (臧斌宇), chair of the OpenHarmony Education Committee, added the talent ledger: more than 100 universities teaching OpenHarmony courses, more than 50,000 students certified, 40-plus university technical clubs, 110-plus developer societies and 50-plus skills workshops across more than 200 universities including two in Southeast Asia, with 33 Nanjing institutions represented on stage and Chen Haibo’s contribution-topic scheme having published 90 topics and seen more than 80 claims211.

Institutional Analysis & Commentary:

Read the four instruments as one regime and the shape is unmistakable: Chinese open source has built a federation — functionally specialised units under a common legal and technical authority — and it administers itself through the instruments cities and companies actually possess. There is no membership treaty. There are four other things.

First, the thing that has been built, named correctly. A federal system needs a common legal vehicle (the foundation), common technical rules (the TOC and the compatibility assessment), a common market (certified products and distributions), fiscal transfers (CNY 150 million a year), subsidiary implementation bodies (the Nanjing centre, the Xihu academy, the Zhengzhou open-source-city mechanism), a subsidy to reduce the cost of joining (Hongtu Plan) and a court of first instance for standards (the assessment-centre network). Every one of those exists. Every one is Chinese. Not one has a foreign seat, a foreign vote or a foreign price. And the units are not each other’s subordinates: Nanjing has a plan and a revenue target, Hangzhou has an academy and a talent scale, Zhengzhou has a city-building agreement, Jiangsu’s vice-governor promises a 15th-Five-Year-Plan software cluster, and the foundation holds the trademark and the certification. This is federalism in the plain sense — divided authority over one common pool — and it is more sophisticated than any single-ministry programme this series has documented.

Second, Ostrom’s collective-choice principle, and why the municipal instrument is where it fails rather than where it is realised. Her eighth design principle is nested enterprises — commons spanning multiple scales should be organised in nested layers, each with rules made by its own participants9. Chinese open source has the nesting. What it lacks is rule-making by the affected. Read the mechanism line again: “enterprise-led, government-guided, university-empowered, alliance-enabled.” Every word names a supplier of inputs — companies supply products, government supplies guidance, universities supply labour, alliances supply coordination. No word names a party whose consent is required. The contribution-topic scheme is the clearest illustration: topics are “published” and then “claimed” (揭榜) — a procurement design, in which the demand side sets the problem and the supply side competes to answer it. That is a sound way to direct funded research. It is not a way to let a contributor decide what the project should be, because that question has already been answered by whoever published the topic.

Third, the compatibility assessment is the load-bearing institution here, and it is a standard with a price rather than a membership with a vote. Count the functions it performs: it defines what counts as an OpenHarmony product (2,600-plus certified, feeding 1.4 billion downstream devices); it allocates market access (a certified product may be sold as “Powered by OpenHarmony”); it is the gate for Hongtu Plan’s assessment incentive — one of the three currencies the programme pays in; and it now has its own network of authorised assessment centres unveiled on stage. This series documented the same instrument’s first mass expiry on 2026-11-21 and the 110,000-odd engineer certificates on 2026-11-24. What changed in Nanjing is not the instrument’s reach but its industrialisation: a service delivered by a network of licensed bodies, funded partly by an adoption incentive, administered by a committee authorised by a board. In Williamson’s terms, governance of this commons has been moved out of the commons and into a franchise — a hierarchy with high asset specificity and a published price. Franchises work. They are also, by construction, permission systems, and the permission-issuer answers to the franchisor.

Fourth, the honest limit of the criticism, stated because the alternative is worse. Nothing in the coverage suggests the assessment regime is corrupt or that its participants are naive; the industry press’s own critique is technical and specific — that agents need toolchains, model adaptation and debugging environments, that chip, module and terminal adaptation consistency is unresolved across brands, that security and trustworthiness cannot rest on enterprise self-attestation, that ecosystem-enterprise counts can be expanded by recruitment while survival depends on stable orders and healthy receivables, and that “if every scenario is highly customised, revenue cannot scale.”13 That is a competent assessment and it is right. What it does not say, and what an institutional reading must, is that a permission system applied to 2,600 products and 120 distributions cannot be evaluated for fairness by any party outside it, because no outsider has standing. Ostrom’s answer to exactly this problem was never “trust the franchisor.” It was a shared forum in which the rules can be contested by the people they bind. The franchise has no such forum. Relocating part of it to a municipal reception centre does not create one; it adds a second franchisor, this one with a revenue target.

Fifth, the comparison the international name invites: two designs, and the rename chooses between them by default if nobody chooses deliberately. Design A — a treaty federation: constituent organisations in multiple jurisdictions, each with legal personality, bound by a published charter, with commitments that bind the centre. This is how the Linux Foundation, Apache, Eclipse and the CNCF actually work: incorporation is the price of admission, and the charter is what the member can sue on. Design B — a domestic federation with an international mission: a single legal entity in one jurisdiction, a foreign-facing posture, foreign partners as guests, and the common rules set by the domestic centre. Design B is cheaper, faster and can do real work — host foreign projects, fund foreign maintainers, export a certification regime. What it cannot do is give a foreign participant a durable claim, because everything it offers is revocable by a board that participant cannot elect, under rules it cannot read, in a jurisdiction it cannot litigate in. The observable difference is a filing: the charter, the foreign entity, or the audit.

Checkable questions.

(i) Is the text of the Nanjing action plan published, with all nine tasks specified and the revenue definition stated — whose revenue, at what margin, counted how? A CNY 100 billion target naming “ecosystem enterprise revenue” is a policy goal; the same number with a defined perimeter is an accountability instrument.

(ii) Which institutions were unveiled as compatibility-assessment partner centres, and is their fee schedule, test suite and pass rate published? The legitimacy of a 2,600-product regime rests entirely on this.

(iii) Does Hongtu Plan’s assessment incentive pay the assessment body or the assessed firm — and is the amount published? That single design choice determines whether the certification market is a market or a rebate.

(iv) Will any non-Chinese entity hold a seat in any of the four bodies — foundation board, TOC, assessment-centre network, Nanjing centre?

(v) Does the “more than 120 distributions” figure reconcile to a published list? Two counts from two parties on one stage, neither defined, is the disclosure pattern this series examined on 2026-11-28.

🔍 Commentary — What a Name Buys, What a Charter Would#

First: the sentence that should be read twice was not about internationalisation at all. It was Xie’s account of the foundation’s own origin — that OpenHarmony was contributed “to the country” in 2020, that the foundation was created by that contribution, and that graduation in 2025 proves the foundation “has the capacity to cultivate open-source projects with international influence.” Read as institutional history, that is precise and candid, and it says something no external critic could say as fairly: the Chinese open-source foundation was built to receive a corporate contribution that its owner decided to make to the state. That is not a scandal and it is not a metaphor — it is a description of who founded whom, and it explains almost everything about the governance that follows. A body created to hold an asset does not spontaneously develop a membership; it develops departments to look after the asset. This one has nine.

Second: the thing the ecosystem keeps building that nobody has asked it to build is a scale for measuring contribution. Zhengzhou priced organisations. Hangzhou levelled individuals. Nanjing has now assigned ten scenario domains, a 100-billion target, and a centre whose job includes “scenario incubation.” Each is a recognition system, each is funded, and none is a rights system. The most striking sentence in today’s material is not from the foundation — it is from Yan Binyu, the education committee chair, on why students come: “because they are clear that an operating system is not a textbook noun, it is a hard craft in the rice bowl of the future.” That is honest, and it states the labour-market motive without varnish. More than 50,000 certified students, 110 developer societies, 33 Nanjing institutions on stage — that is real institutional capacity, built in six years, and a reader who dismisses it is making a category error. What it is capacity for is the only question. A person who has been certified, and whose certification is recognised by an employer incentivised by a programme that pays in three currencies, has been given a career. They have not been given standing in the repository.

Third: the structural asymmetry, and why “international” matters more this week than last. This series has argued since October that the Chinese ecosystem builds instruments that govern artefacts — licences, standards, certifications, export filings, recognition ceremonies — and never the instrument that governs a commons, a membership with a vote. Today’s announcement tests whether that still holds at the moment the word enters the legal name. State the optimistic reading in full, because it is available: a foundation that may now fund and contract abroad is a foundation that can pay a foreign maintainer, host a foreign project, and sign a charter a foreign party could enforce. If that happens, the ecosystem will have solved in one administrative act the problem it has not solved domestically in six years — because foreign law will require the charter to be real. That is not a cynical reading; it is how institutions usually learn. The pessimistic reading is that “international” is a mission-statement word, the funding stays domestic, certification stays a domestic franchise, and by 2028 the foundation is larger, better funded and no more open to a stranger’s patch than it is today. Both readings are consistent with everything published on 2026-11-28. They diverge in a filing, and the filing arrives in August.

Fourth: for an outside reader — a foreign maintainer, an enterprise procurement officer, a policy researcher — the practical guidance is unchanged and should be said plainly. OpenHarmony’s artefacts are real: 150 million lines, 2,600-plus certified products, 1.4 billion units of downstream hardware, 120-plus distributions, a compatibility regime with its own assessment network. Anything you build on them, you can also check. What you cannot check is the governance, because there is no published charter, no membership you can join, no vote you can cast, and — as of this morning — the renamed foundation does not yet say so on its own website. The posture is the one this series recommended on 2026-11-28 in a different context: buy the artefact, verify it yourself, and do not treat a licence or a title as a promise. A permissive licence today is a promise about yesterday’s file.

Fifth: what to watch, all of it checkable within two quarters.

  • The registry change and the English name. When openatom.org reads “OpenAtom International Open Source Foundation,” the registration has happened; until then the announcement is a decision.
  • The 章程 (charter), published. The single document that would convert every criticism in this briefing into an answerable question.
  • A membership class with an admission route and a vote, and whether it admits any non-Chinese person.
  • The 2026 audit report, due on the foundation’s own audit page: look for a foreign entity, a grant outside mainland China, or an international-expenditure line.
  • The Nanjing centre’s first year: named scenarios, named firms, and whether the nine-task document is public.
  • And the proxy this series has used all November, which no rename addresses: a merged patch from outside the founding company, with a maintainer named in a CODEOWNERS file. The only test that costs nothing to run and cannot be met by announcement.
